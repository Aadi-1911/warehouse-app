# Learning Log

Three things live here: **Decisions & Reasoning** (why we built things the way we did, what we didn't do instead, and why), **Mistakes & Fixes** (real problems hit during the build, how they were diagnosed, and why the actual fix is correct — not just a workaround), and a **Concepts** glossary. This file grows continuously as the actual build happens — the moment a real decision gets made, something breaks, or a new concept comes up, even mid-task. Covers backend and frontend equally. Concept entries aim to be complete enough to actually understand the idea, including whatever context it depends on, not just a one-line definition. See CLAUDE.md for the full instruction. This file is the "why" (and "what went wrong") behind everything; the other numbered `.md` docs in this folder are the "what."

## Decisions & Reasoning

### Why this business, this way (the founding idea)
The project serves two goals at once: a real tool for a real wholesale garment business, and a genuine full-stack learning project. When they conflict, the business wins — this was stated explicitly early on ("if it's boring, it should be a win for the business, not the resume"). This single rule ended up settling almost every later scope argument without needing a fresh debate each time.

### Why Phase 1 is Inventory only, not the full system
The full picture that emerged through design (Inventory + Parties + Orders + Billing + Payments) is, honestly, a lightweight ERP — a much bigger build than "an inventory app." Rather than building all of it before shipping anything, we cut to the single piece that mattered most *right now*, confirmed directly rather than assumed. Building everything at once would have meant months of planning with nothing real in daily use, and no early feedback to catch mistakes cheaply. Shipping Inventory alone, in real use, teaches more per hour than a bigger unfinished build ever would.

### Why React is kept separate from the backend (not Next.js)
Next.js would merge frontend and backend into one project — less boilerplate, but it hides the actual HTTP request/response boundary between client and server. Keeping them as two separate projects forces genuine understanding of what a REST API is, why CORS exists, and how a real client talks to a real server — foundational, transferable knowledge that a merged framework lets you skip entirely. The cost (a bit more deployment complexity, two services instead of one) is small next to what's gained.

### Why Prisma over raw SQL
Raw SQL (writing every query and migration by hand) teaches the most, but costs the most time for a total beginner — a real risk against a job-hunting timeline. Prisma turned out to be the rare case where the *easier* option isn't actually a shortcut: the schema file is basically a formalized version of the entity model already designed by hand, migrations are generated and inspectable, and Prisma Studio gives a real GUI into the data. The habit that keeps it honest: periodically turn on query logging and read the SQL it generates, so it never becomes a total black box.

### Why auth is hand-rolled, not a managed service (Clerk/Auth0/etc.)
At 2–5 users, a managed auth service doesn't save much real time — and the app has a genuinely non-standard requirement (role check *plus* a separate PIN specifically for price edits) that doesn't fit a generic login SDK's assumptions anyway. Rolling it means real, interview-relevant understanding of password hashing, sessions, and protected routes, for roughly the same effort as fighting a tool that wasn't built for this shape of requirement.

### Why a website (PWA), not a native app
Native apps earn their complexity when they unlock something a browser genuinely can't do well — offline-first sync, camera/barcode scanning, heavy push notifications. None of those are actually needed here: connectivity in the field is mostly reliable (occasional drops fall back to manual re-entry), and there's no barcode-scanning workflow in how articles get selected. A skilled team with unlimited resources would build the same thing — a responsive PWA — because nothing about the actual business creates an advantage for native at this scale.

### Why Bundle = Product + Color, and sizes are fixed per Product
Early assumption was that sizes might need tracking per bundle or per color. Stress-testing against how the business actually operates revealed the size range is always the same for a given article — a different size range simply gets a different Article No. entirely. This *simplified* the model rather than complicating it: sizes belong once, at the Product level, not duplicated everywhere a color or bundle appears.

### Why Stock is a join table (Bundle × Location), not a single number per Bundle
A single stock count per Bundle couldn't represent the real situation: the same Bundle (e.g. Article A101 in Navy) can have different quantities sitting in Delhi versus Gurgaon. Splitting Stock out as its own table, keyed on both Bundle and Location, was the only way to represent that truthfully.

### Why cost_price/sellingPrice are nullable ("pending price"), not required at creation
Forcing a price to be set the moment an article is created would block staff from logging a receiving session for a genuinely new article until the owner was available — unrealistic given staff and owner are never even on the same device (phone vs. PC). Making price nullable let receiving and pricing become two independent steps: staff can log what physically arrived immediately, and a "pending price" badge naturally signals what still needs the owner's attention, without inventing a separate approval workflow.

### Why price editing needs a PIN, not just the owner role
This one got revised. The first answer was "owner-role login is enough" — but that quietly dropped an explicit original requirement (owner wanted protection "even for himself," given how sensitive cost data is). Revisiting it: role alone proves *who's logged in*, not that *this specific action* was intentional. The PIN is a second, action-specific confirmation — small cost, but it restores a requirement that almost got lost in simplification.

### Why Order status is four stages, not three
A prototyping tool's demo collapsed this to Open → Packed → Shipped, skipping "Billed" entirely. But billing is a genuinely separate, harder problem (a real invoice document, GST fields, e-way compliance) that hasn't even been designed yet — collapsing it into "shipped" would hide that dependency and make it easy to accidentally ship something that was never properly billed. Keeping Billed as its own stage keeps that gap visible instead of papered over.

### Why "adjusted" is a logged event, not a status value
Orders can change at any point — before packing, during packing, even after, per how the business actually operates. Modeling that as a status (`Placed → Adjusted → Packed`) would require illegal-looking backward transitions once packing had already started. Instead, status stays simple and linear (Placed → Packed → Billed → Shipped), and every change is a separate append-only log entry layered on top — the full history is visible without corrupting the simple state machine.

### Why payments default to FIFO but allow a mandatory-reasoned override
Most payments are simple — pay the oldest bill first. But real Parties don't always pay against the bill you'd expect. Forcing pure FIFO would misrepresent reality; allowing free-form override with no record would make it untraceable. Requiring a note *specifically when* someone overrides the default keeps the common case frictionless while keeping every exception explainable later.

### Why the "outstanding amount" tracker is separate from the real Bill
Full invoice generation (matching the business's actual Excel template, GST fields, e-way thresholds, immutability, corrections) is a genuinely bigger, harder, still-undesigned problem. But the owner needs *some* visibility into who owes what well before that's built. Splitting them meant shipping real value now without waiting on — or accidentally under-designing — the harder piece.

### Why Kids sizing reuses the adult sizing rule instead of a new 4pc/6pc lookup table
The obvious approach would hardcode "kids sets are 4 or 6 pieces." But the adult rule (pieces-per-set = however many size options got selected) already generalizes perfectly to age brackets — 4 brackets selected = a 4pc set, 6 selected = a 6pc set. Recognizing the existing rule already covered the new case avoided inventing and maintaining a second, redundant system.

### Why low-stock is a small badge, never a fully-tinted card
An early version tinted the whole card/row for any shortfall. In practice, that trains people to tune out red over time (alarm fatigue) — and makes genuinely urgent situations blend in with routine ones. A small flag, reserved for a specific threshold (≤2 sets), stays meaningful precisely because it's rare.

### Why session items snapshot their Factory/Location/price instead of reading it live
If a receiving session's item just referenced the session-level Factory/Location dropdown, changing that dropdown mid-session (e.g. realizing halfway through that goods are actually going to a different warehouse) would silently corrupt every item already entered. Capturing those values on each item *at the moment it's finalized* means later changes can't reach backward and corrupt earlier data — a real bug class avoided by design rather than caught by testing.

### Why jsonwebtoken/express-session weren't installed yet
`02_ARCHITECTURE.md` §4.1 explicitly leaves session-vs-JWT as an open choice ("either is acceptable, pick one"). That's a real auth-design decision, and building auth is its own future task — so the backend-init task (npm init + minimal server) only installed what's needed for *any* server to exist (express, cors, dotenv, bcrypt, prisma), not the auth-token library itself. Avoids default-picking an approach that should get a deliberate answer when we actually build login.

### Why cors and dotenv were added even though the doc only names Express/Prisma/bcrypt
Neither is a business-logic choice — they're plumbing required by decisions already made elsewhere in the doc: `cors` exists because frontend and backend are separate deployments (§3), so the browser needs an explicit "this origin is allowed" signal or every request gets blocked. `dotenv` exists because the doc's own env var list (§7) has to be loaded into the app somehow. Neither adds a new capability beyond what was already decided; they just implement it.

### Why the schema was transcribed from 03_DATABASE_SCHEMA.md §1 rather than redesigned
That document already *is* the finalized Phase 1 data model — the entity design work happened when it was written, not now. Copying it verbatim (including its own inline comments) avoided silently second-guessing decisions already made, and kept that task scoped to "get the schema into the database," not "redesign the schema."

### Why the schema alone doesn't enforce cost_price visibility, the PIN gate, or atomic stock writes
Prisma's schema language can describe *shape* (a `Decimal?` field can hold null or a number) but not *behavior* (who's allowed to read a field, or that a write must happen alongside an audit row). Those four rules — no `cost_price` to STAFF, PIN + OWNER role to edit prices, Stock only changes via a `Transaction` insert, Bundle/Color pairs validated before accepting a transaction — all have to be checked in the Express controllers/middleware that sit in front of Prisma, not in the schema file itself. The schema just makes the *right* shape possible (e.g. `Stock.qtySets` has no API to edit it directly yet — there's no route for that at all).

### Why the database was checked for existing tables before migrating
`prisma migrate dev` can be destructive if it detects drift against an already-populated database (it may prompt to reset). Since the Neon database was freshly provisioned, checked first with `prisma migrate diff --script` (prints the SQL Prisma would run without applying it) — confirming it was 100% `CREATE TABLE`/`CREATE TYPE` with no `ALTER`/`DROP` statements, meaning the database was genuinely empty and there was nothing at risk of being overwritten.

### Why History edits create a new entry instead of overwriting in place
The founding principle for the whole system is that every number traces back to a logged event — nothing is ever just manually edited with no trace. A literal in-place edit (even with an "(edited)" tag) still destroys the original value. A new correcting entry costs a little more complexity but keeps that principle intact even when accommodating real mistakes.

### Why JWT was chosen over session-based auth (02_ARCHITECTURE.md §4.1)
The deciding factor was the deployment topology, not a general preference: frontend (Vercel) and backend (Railway/Render) are separately deployed, almost certainly different origins in production. Session auth needs a cookie sent back on every request, and a cookie shared across two different origins needs `SameSite=None; Secure` plus matching CORS `credentials: true` on both sides — a genuinely fiddly cross-site configuration, especially on mobile Safari/PWA (the staff device profile). It would also need a server-side session store so sessions survive a server restart. JWT sidesteps both: the client carries its own signed token via an `Authorization: Bearer` header, no cookies or session store needed at all — and it reinforces the same "explicit client-server boundary" lesson that's the whole reason frontend/backend are kept separate in the first place (§3).

### Why the auth middleware re-fetches the user's role instead of trusting the JWT payload
Textbook JWT usage trusts whatever role is embedded in the token, since that's the whole "stateless" point of JWTs. But `cost_price` visibility is the one rule CLAUDE.md calls non-negotiable "under any circumstance" — a still-valid old token must not keep granting a role the user no longer has (e.g. if an account got demoted or disabled after the token was issued). So the token only carries `userId`; the middleware looks up that user's *current* role from the database on every request. This costs one extra DB read per request, which is free at 2–5 users, in exchange for the role check always reflecting reality.

### Why the price-edit PIN is a 6-digit bcrypt hash, not a 4-digit or plaintext one
Reused `bcrypt` (already in the codebase for the login password) rather than adding a second hashing library for a second secret on the same `User` model — no real reason to diverge. Length was an open choice the docs didn't specify: went with 6 digits (1,000,000 combinations) over the more common 4-digit ATM-style PIN (10,000), because unlike a bank PIN this one isn't backed by a physical card that gets seized after a few wrong tries — the only real defense here is entropy plus rate limiting together, so it was worth the small extra typing cost.

### Why PIN rate limiting is per-user and DB-backed, not IP-based or in-memory
IP-based limiting is the wrong shape for this threat model — it wouldn't stop an attacker using a stolen JWT from the owner's own network, and with a single OWNER account there's no meaningful "per-IP" pattern to distinguish attacker from owner. Tracking `failedPinAttempts`/`pinLockedUntil` directly on the `User` row instead means the lockout is tied to the specific account being attacked, and survives a server restart or a future move to multiple backend instances — an in-memory counter would silently reset on either.

### Why the seed script backfills the dev PIN only if missing, instead of always setting it
The OWNER row already existed from the previous task's seed run, before this PIN feature existed — a plain re-seed wouldn't touch it, since `passwordHash` and now `priceEditPinHash` are only ever set once (at creation, or via this one-time backfill), never overwritten by re-running the script. This matters beyond just fixing today's already-existing row: once a real "change PIN" endpoint exists, re-running `prisma db seed` (e.g. during a fresh environment setup) must never silently revert the owner's real PIN back to the `123456` dev default.

### Why costPrice omission uses Prisma's `select`, not fetch-everything-then-delete-a-key
Both approaches can produce identical JSON for a STAFF response today. The difference shows up under future change: if some later route did `res.json({ ...product, extra })`, a fetch-everything approach would silently leak `costPrice` back in the moment anyone forgot to re-delete it, while a `select`-based approach means `costPrice` was never fetched from Postgres in the first place for a STAFF request — there is no key to accidentally reintroduce, because it was never in the JS object at all. Given CLAUDE.md's "under any circumstance" wording for this specific rule, the version that's structurally incapable of the leak (not just currently-correct) was worth the small extra `productSelect(role)` indirection.

### Why the API's error responses were retrofitted to `{ error: { code, message } }` mid-task
`auth.js`, `authController.js`, `requireRole.js`, and `requirePin.js` were all built in earlier tasks using a flatter `{ error: "string" }` shape and somewhat improvised status codes (e.g. 400 for a missing PIN, 423 for a lockout) — reasonable guesses at the time, but built before `04_API_SPEC.md`'s "General Error Conventions" section had been read closely. That section is explicit that *all* error responses share one shape, and specifically classifies "missing/invalid PIN" under `403`, not 400/401/423. Since this task's whole point was wiring `requireRole`/`requirePin` into a real spec-driven endpoint, shipping new endpoints that were spec-correct on top of old middleware that wasn't would have produced an inconsistent API on day one — cheaper to fix the four existing files now than to leave two different error shapes coexisting.

### Why POST duplicate-article and missing-factory checks catch Prisma errors instead of pre-checking existence
A "check if it exists, then insert" pattern has a race window — two near-simultaneous requests could both pass the check before either insert lands, and the database's own unique constraint would still catch it, just after doing unnecessary work. Instead, `createProduct` attempts the insert directly and catches Prisma's `P2002` (unique constraint violated → 409 duplicate article) and `P2003` (foreign key violated → 404 factory not found) — the database's constraints are already the actual source of truth for these rules (`@@unique([articleNo, factoryId])`, the `factoryId` foreign key), so there's no separate check to keep in sync with them or race against.

### Why PATCH rejects articleNo/factoryId instead of silently allowing or ignoring them
`04_API_SPEC.md` doesn't enumerate exactly which fields are PATCH-able ("any subset of editable fields... category, etc."). Silently ignoring an attempt to change `factoryId` would be confusing — a client sending `{ factoryId: 'x' }` and getting a 200 with no visible effect looks like a bug, not a deliberate rule. Explicitly rejecting with 400 makes the constraint visible: changing either field after creation could violate the per-factory article-uniqueness rule or orphan Bundles/Stock rows still tied to the original factory, so it's excluded on purpose, not by omission.

### Why POST /api/locations was built as OWNER-only, even though the task described it as "any authenticated role"
`04_API_SPEC.md` explicitly marks `POST /api/factories` and `POST /api/colors` as 🔒 (any role) but `POST /api/locations` as 👑 (OWNER only) — a real, specific difference from the other two, not a typo skimmed past. Since the doc is the named source of truth for this task ("build...from 04_API_SPEC.md"), built it as the doc states and flagged the discrepancy rather than silently following the paraphrase or silently following the doc without saying anything — either silent choice would have left a real mismatch undiscovered.

### Why Color/Location uniqueness uses a case-insensitive app-level check plus a P2002 catch, not a case-insensitive database index
The schema's `name @unique` on both models is a plain Postgres unique index, which is case-sensitive by default — "Navy" and "navy" wouldn't collide at the DB level alone. A fully database-enforced case-insensitive constraint is possible (a functional index on `LOWER(name)`, or the `citext` extension) but needs hand-written SQL in the migration, extra complexity for what `04_API_SPEC.md` itself only calls "recommended" on a low-traffic lookup table with 2–5 users. A `findFirst` with `mode: 'insensitive'` before the insert, plus catching `P2002` as a backup for the exact-case race, is proportionate to the actual stakes here — unlike `costPrice` visibility, a duplicate color slipping through under rare concurrent-request timing is a minor cleanup annoyance, not a rule CLAUDE.md calls non-negotiable.

### Why Location got the same case-insensitive treatment as Color, though the spec only says it for Color
`04_API_SPEC.md`'s case-insensitivity language ("recommended, to avoid 'Navy' vs 'navy' duplicates") appears only in the Colors section, not Locations. But Location names are the same shape of problem — a short free-text field ("Delhi" vs "delhi") — so applying the same pattern for consistency seemed like the more helpful default. Flagging this explicitly since it's an extension by analogy, not something the spec literally states for Locations.

### Why Bundle creation pre-checks Product/Color existence instead of only catching a Prisma FK error
Products' `createProduct` catches `P2003` for its one foreign key (`factoryId`) and that's unambiguous — there's only one thing it could be. Bundles have *two* foreign keys (`productId`, `colorId`), and a bare `P2003` doesn't cleanly identify which one failed without fragile parsing of Prisma's error internals. Two explicit `findUnique` existence checks before the create cost two extra queries but give an unambiguous `PRODUCT_NOT_FOUND` vs `COLOR_NOT_FOUND` — worth it here specifically because of the two-FK shape, not a blanket departure from the catch-the-constraint pattern used everywhere else (the actual uniqueness rule, `(productId, colorId)`, still gets caught via `P2002`, same as Colors/Locations/Products).

### Why POST /api/transactions guards the negative-quantity check inside the UPDATE's WHERE clause, not with a separate read-then-check
This is the endpoint every Stock number in the app flows through, so it got more care than a typical CRUD write. The naive version — read `qtySets`, check in JS whether subtracting would go negative, then write the computed value — has a real race: two near-simultaneous requests against the same Stock row could both read the same starting value, both pass the check, and both write, letting stock go negative despite the check technically existing. Instead, `buildStockUpdate` produces a Prisma `updateMany` with the guard baked into the `where` clause itself (e.g. `where: { id, qtySets: { gte: qtySets } }` combined with `data: { qtySets: { decrement: qtySets } }`) — this compiles to one atomic SQL `UPDATE ... WHERE qtySets >= ?`, so the database evaluates the guard against the true current value at the exact moment it applies the write. A `count: 0` result means the guard failed, which is how the negative case gets detected — there's no window where two requests can both pass a stale check.

### Why the whole find-or-create-then-update-then-insert sequence runs inside one `$transaction` callback, not the array form
Prisma's array form (`$transaction([op1, op2])`) only works for a fixed, pre-determined list of operations — it can't express "read something, then conditionally decide to error out based on that read." The callback form (`$transaction(async (tx) => {...})`) runs everything on one real Postgres transaction, and throwing inside it rolls back *everything* the callback did so far — including the Stock row that the find-or-create step might have just created moments earlier for a bundle/location with no prior history. That specific case mattered concretely: attempting `STOCK_OUT` against a never-before-touched bundle/location creates a zero-value Stock row via `upsert`, then immediately fails the negative guard — without the rollback, that phantom zero-value row would persist even though the actual transaction was rejected. Verified this directly by counting `Transaction` rows after 3 deliberately-rejected attempts sandwiched between 4 real ones — exactly 4 exist, not 7, and Stock quantities matched exactly what the 4 successful operations should have produced.

### Why the catch-all error handler shows the real error message in dev but a generic one in production
Every other error in this API is deliberate — the code already knows exactly what's wrong (a duplicate name, a missing field) and `sendError()` names it specifically, matching `04_API_SPEC.md`'s "never generic 'something went wrong'" guidance. This handler is the opposite case by definition: it only fires when nothing upstream understood what happened, which means the raw `err.message` could contain anything, including internal details (stack traces, DB connection specifics, file paths) that shouldn't go out over the network to a client. Gating on `NODE_ENV` (already a tracked env var) gets both: real detail for local development, where seeing the actual message is genuinely useful, and a safe generic message once deployed, while the full error is `console.error`'d server-side either way so nothing is actually lost, just not exposed to the caller.

### Why GET /api/stock's response is a flattened, joined shape instead of the raw Stock row
`04_API_SPEC.md` specifies display-ready strings (`productArticleNo`, `colorName`, `locationName`) alongside `bundleId`/`locationId`, not the raw `Bundle`/`Product`/`Color`/`Location` records a Stock row actually relates to. This is a deliberate join, not a shortcut: the Live Stock View is meant to render a search/filter table directly from this response, and making the frontend separately fetch Products/Colors/Locations just to label each row would be real, avoidable extra work.

**Update:** the first version of this endpoint was built when the spec's response shape only listed `locationName`, not `locationId` — flagged at the time as a likely gap, since a client wanting to fire `POST /api/transactions` from a stock row needs the real `locationId`, not just its display name. `04_API_SPEC.md` was updated to add `locationId` to the documented response, and the endpoint (plus its test's field-set assertion) was updated to match. Worth remembering as a small case study in the doc-first workflow this project uses: flagging a suspected gap rather than silently patching around it meant the spec doc itself got corrected, not just this one endpoint's implementation quietly diverging from what's written.

### Why /api/users reuses the exact same "select, don't strip" pattern as costPrice
`GET /api/users` and `POST /api/users`'s responses never SELECT `passwordHash`/`priceEditPinHash` from the database at all, the same approach `productController.js` uses to keep `costPrice` away from STAFF — not "fetch the full row, then delete the sensitive keys before responding." Consistency mattered here specifically because these two fields are the actual credential material for every account in the system (a password hash and the price-edit PIN hash) — genuinely the highest-stakes fields in the whole schema to accidentally leak, more so than `costPrice`. Using the identical, already-proven pattern rather than inventing a new one for this endpoint keeps the guarantee structural in exactly the same way: there's no key to leak because it was never fetched, not because someone remembered to strip it.

### Why GET /api/products/:id/valid-colors lives in productController.js/routes/products.js, not its own file
The URL is `/api/products/:id/valid-colors` — a sub-resource of a specific Product, not an independent Bundle-listing endpoint (that's already `GET /api/bundles?productId=`, built earlier, which returns a different, un-joined shape). Since Express routes on path structure, not on which "resource" a route conceptually belongs to, mounting it as `router.get('/:id/valid-colors', ...)` alongside the other `/api/products/*` routes keeps the URL structure and the file structure in sync, rather than splitting one product's sub-paths across two different route files for no functional reason.

### Why GET /api/transactions' response shape includes locationName/locationId even though the spec's prose only says "bundle/product/color info"
Unlike most other endpoints, `04_API_SPEC.md` describes this response only in prose ("joined user name, bundle/product/color info for display"), not as a literal object — a real design decision, not a transcription. Included `locationName`/`locationId` anyway: the endpoint has a `?locationId=` filter, and filtering by something the response never displays would be a strange asymmetry — a history/audit view is close to meaningless without knowing *where* each entry happened. Otherwise reused the exact field names already established for `GET /api/stock` (`productArticleNo`, `colorName`, `locationName`) for the same underlying joined data, plus the raw IDs alongside each display string (`userId`+`userName`, `bundleId`+`productArticleNo`/`colorName`, `locationId`+`locationName`) — the same "IDs for linking, strings for display" shape `GET /api/stock` ended up with after its own `locationId` addition. Also defaulted to newest-first ordering (`createdAt: desc`), not stated in the spec but the standard expectation for an audit list — flagged all of this explicitly since none of it is spec-literal.

### Why the JWT is stored in localStorage, not sessionStorage, in-memory, or an httpOnly cookie
`02_ARCHITECTURE.md` deliberately leaves this open, and all four options have real costs. **httpOnly cookie** is the strongest against XSS — JavaScript genuinely cannot read it — but the backend is already built around `Authorization: Bearer` headers, and more importantly cookies are sent *automatically* by the browser, which is precisely what makes them CSRF-vulnerable; Bearer tokens are naturally CSRF-immune because an attacker's page can't make the browser attach a header it doesn't know. Going cookie-based would trade an XSS risk for a CSRF risk plus genuine cross-origin cookie complexity (`SameSite=None; Secure` + CORS credentials, since Vercel and Railway are different domains). **In-memory (React state)** loses the session on every refresh, and its security gain is only partial — script that can run arbitrary JS can hook `fetch` or walk React's fiber tree to reach the token anyway. Staff run this as a phone PWA that gets backgrounded constantly, so they'd be logged out repeatedly during the core daily workflow: a *certain, daily* usability cost for a *partial* defense against an *unlikely* threat. **sessionStorage** is the trap option — identical XSS exposure to localStorage (any script reads it), but logged out on tab close and not shared between tabs, so it's pure friction for zero gain against the actual threat. Chose **localStorage**, with the tradeoff stated honestly: XSS means token theft. What makes that acceptable *here specifically* is three things — the attack surface is small (internal tool, no user-generated HTML rendered, React escapes by default, and `dangerouslySetInnerHTML` is banned in this codebase); `auth.js` re-fetches the user from the database every request instead of trusting token claims, so a stolen token grants exactly that account's real current permissions and tampering achieves nothing; and the PIN gate means a stolen token *still* cannot edit prices, because the PIN is entered fresh per edit. The architecture already assumes the session token isn't sufficient for the highest-stakes action, which is what makes this a defensible choice rather than a lazy one. In a banking app the calculus flips entirely.

### Why only the token is persisted, and the user object is re-fetched from the server on load
The alternative — storing `{ token, user }` together in localStorage — avoids a network round-trip on every page load. Rejected it because `user.role` decides what the account is allowed to see, and localStorage is trivially editable in devtools: persisting the role would mean the client holds a copy of its own permissions. Instead `AuthProvider` stores only the token and calls `GET /api/auth/me` on mount to re-derive the user from the server. The cost is one request per load; the benefits are that the role always reflects current server truth (an account changed from OWNER to STAFF takes effect on next load, not whenever the stale copy expires), and the call doubles as a liveness check — an expired token fails at load time rather than surfacing mid-action later. This is the frontend mirror of the backend decision to re-fetch the role instead of trusting the JWT payload: neither side treats client-held data as authoritative about permissions.

### Why auth has three states (loading/authenticated/unauthenticated), not an `isLoggedIn` boolean
On a hard refresh there's a real third state: a token exists but hasn't been validated yet. A boolean has nowhere to put that, so it collapses into `false` — and `ProtectedRoute` would redirect to `/login` for the split second before `/me` resolves, producing a login-screen flash on every single refresh, then a bounce back. The three-state version gives `loading` its own branch that renders a placeholder and redirects for neither outcome. Verified this concretely rather than assuming: sampling the DOM ~40ms into a fresh load with a valid stored token shows `"Loading…"` at path `/`, not the login form — the exact moment the boolean version would have already navigated away.

### Why the API client dispatches a `session-expired` event instead of just clearing the token
When the server rejects a token mid-session (7-day expiry lapsing, or the account being deleted), `client.js` clears localStorage — but clearing storage doesn't re-render React, so the UI would keep showing a logged-in shell whose every subsequent request fails. Rather than have every caller handle this, `client.js` fires a window event that `AuthProvider` listens for and mirrors into React state, so the app actually logs out. The clear is guarded on a token having *been sent*: a failed login also returns 401, but there's no session to tear down in that case, and firing the event there would be wrong.

### Why the Home footer note renders for STAFF only, though the brief lists it unconditionally
`07_UI_DESIGN_BRIEF.md` §5.1 specifies the bottom banner as "pricing/article setup is owner-only and **hidden from this account**." That final clause only makes sense read by a staff member — telling an owner that pricing is hidden from them is simply false, since the PIN-gated price edit is *their* screen. The brief resolves this itself in §2: "All screens in this brief are for the Staff role" — so its screens are written from the staff perspective throughout, and applying them verbatim to an owner isn't what it's asking for. Rendered it behind `user.role === 'STAFF'`. Worth being clear about what this note is and isn't: it's an *explanation*, not a control. The actual enforcement is server-side (`productSelect(role)` omits `costPrice` from the SQL query itself for STAFF) and stands whether or not this paragraph is on screen.

### Why Home shows two tiles rather than the brief's four
The brief specifies a 2×2 grid of Receive Stock / New Order / Pack Order / Live Stock. New Order and Pack Order depend on Party and Order entities, which `06_ROADMAP.md` places in Phase 2 — their screens don't exist and their endpoints aren't built. Rendering them as disabled or dead tiles would advertise functionality that isn't there. Kept the CSS grid at two columns rather than collapsing to a single column for two tiles: the Phase 2 pair will wrap onto a second row and produce the specified 2×2 with no layout change, so the current state is a subset of the final design rather than a different design that needs undoing.

### Why tiles are `<Link>` elements rather than buttons with click handlers
Both are technically capable of navigating. An anchor is what a browser already understands as navigation, so middle-click and long-press "open in new tab" keep working, the destination shows on hover, and assistive technology announces it as a link. A `<button onClick={() => navigate(...)}>` reimplements a subset of that and silently drops the rest. Same reasoning as preferring a real `<form onSubmit>` over a click handler on the Login screen — using the element the platform already defines for the job means inheriting its behaviour instead of rebuilding it.

### Why icons are hand-written inline SVG rather than an icon library
The screens specified across the whole brief need on the order of a dozen icons. A library (lucide, react-icons) would be more convenient, but adds a runtime dependency for something a few `<path>` elements cover. That tradeoff leans further than usual toward "no dependency" in this project specifically, because the JWT lives in localStorage — an XSS hole means token theft, and a compromised transitive dependency is the realistic route to XSS in an app that renders no user-supplied HTML (see the localStorage decision above). Every icon strokes with `currentColor`, so it inherits its container's role tint instead of hard-coding a hex, which is what lets one component satisfy §3.1's "background, border and icon share one role" rule wherever it's placed.

### Why the article-lookup match is filtered client-side instead of trusting the backend's `?articleNo=` filter
`GET /api/products?articleNo=` (built in an earlier task, for the Live Stock search box) does a case-insensitive *contains* match — the right behaviour for a search field, where "search as you type" should surface partial hits. The Receive Stock lookup needs something different: an *exact* answer to "does this specific article number already exist for this Factory," since `(articleNo, factoryId)` is a unique constraint in the schema — there can only ever be zero or one real match, never a list to browse. Reusing the endpoint as-is would return false "Matched" positives (searching "RCV0" would match "RCV01") or, worse, an ambiguous result if two unrelated articles happened to share a substring. `api/products.js`'s `findExactMatch()` calls the existing endpoint (no backend change needed) and then filters its results down to a genuine case-insensitive equality check before deciding Matched vs New. Kept the backend endpoint unchanged deliberately — it's still correct for its original caller, and this task's scope was the frontend screen, not that endpoint.

### Why selecting a new Factory clears any lookup already on screen

If a Matched or New-article banner from the old Factory stayed on screen after switching, it would silently become stale and wrong: a "Matched" result computed against Factory A has no bearing on Factory B. `handleFactoryChange` resets the lookup state on every Factory change, forcing a fresh (correct) lookup rather than trusting a result that no longer describes the current selection. Verified this concretely, not just assumed: the test looks up the same real article number under two different Factories and confirms it resolves to Matched under one and New under the other.

### Why color staging only exists under the Matched banner, not the New-article one
The task's own requirement ("Color dropdown scoped via `GET /api/products/:id/valid-colors`") only has a real target for the Matched branch — that endpoint needs a `Product.id`, and a New article doesn't have one until it's actually created (Name+Sizes fields, a separate not-yet-built task). ### Why "switch color" and "Add to receipt" share one `finalizeActiveColor` helper instead of two separate implementations
Both moments need to answer the exact same question — "does the color currently being set up (picked, with some sets on it) deserve to become a permanent staged entry?" — just triggered by different events. Writing that logic twice would let the two copies drift (e.g. one enforcing "sets > 0" and the other forgetting to), and rule 51 (a colour "not explicitly added must still be included... not silently dropped") applies to both triggers identically. `finalizeActiveColor` takes the current staged array and returns what it should become, called from both `handleColorChange` and `handleAddToReceipt` — the single source of truth for "when does an in-progress pick become real."

### Why "at least one staged color has sets > 0" (the task's literal wording) needed reconciling with rule 51
Read literally, the task implies checking `stagedColors.length > 0` alone. But rule 51 requires the *currently active, not-yet-switched-away-from* color to also count if it has sets — otherwise a staff member who picks one color, sets 3, and clicks "Add to receipt" immediately (without ever triggering a color-switch) would find the button still disabled, which contradicts "must still be included... not silently dropped." `canAddToReceipt` therefore checks `stagedColors.length > 0 || (selectedColorId && currentSets > 0)` — both the already-staged list AND the pending active pick. Flagged this explicitly rather than silently picking the narrower literal reading, since it changes real button-enablement behavior.

### Why changing Location now resets the lookup, mirroring Factory — a gap found while designing this task, not asked for directly
Only Factory's `onChange` reset the in-progress lookup before this task; Location's didn't. That was fine when there was nothing to lose on a Location change (no staging existed yet), but it becomes a real bug once color staging exists: a staff member could match an article, stage colors under Location A, then absent-mindedly change the Location dropdown to B, and "Add to receipt" would commit the entry with `locationId` now pointing at B — silently contradicting rule 52's snapshot guarantee (which exists specifically to prevent a later dropdown change from corrupting an in-progress entry). Extended `handleLocationChange` to call the same `resetLookup()` Factory already used, closing the gap symmetrically rather than leaving an inconsistency that would only surface as a real data-correctness bug once someone actually reaches for it.

### Why the color dropdown filters out already-staged colors instead of allowing reselect-and-merge
Once a color is staged, letting it stay selectable would mean deciding what happens if it's picked again — merge the new sets into the existing entry? Overwrite? Reject? Filtering it out of `availableColors` (`validColors` minus anything already in `stagedColors`) sidesteps the question entirely: a color can only ever be "active" or "staged," never both, so there's no merge case to get right or wrong. The only way to touch an already-staged color again is to remove it first (the × control), which puts it back in the selectable list with a clean slate.

### Why receiptItems isn't rendered as anything resembling the real receipt table
The task explicitly listed "the receipt table's final grouped rendering" as out of scope, and rule 53 specifies real requirements for that table (grouped by article, showing sets *and pieces* per color line) that this task never asked for the data to support. Rendering committed items in any real form risked either half-building that table now (rework later) or quietly deciding its shape without being asked to. Settled on a single muted count line ("N articles added to this receipt so far") — enough to prove commits are landing in a real browser, deliberately not resembling a table. Flagged this choice explicitly rather than assuming it was wanted, since "commit into the session-level receipt list" only strictly required the *state* to exist, not any particular rendering of it.

### Why POST /api/products' select had to change from a hardcoded 'OWNER' to `productSelect(req.user.role)` when the role gate loosened
Before this change, `createProduct` was only reachable by OWNER (`requireRole('OWNER')` on the route), so hardcoding `select: productSelect('OWNER')` for the response was harmless — every caller who could reach the function already had OWNER-level visibility anyway. The moment the route opened to any authenticated role, that hardcoding became a live leak: a STAFF request creating a Product would get back a response selected as if it were OWNER, handing `costPrice` straight to a STAFF-role request — exactly the case CLAUDE.md's non-negotiable rule exists to prevent, and exactly the kind of bug the "select, don't strip" pattern is supposed to make structurally impossible elsewhere. Caught by rereading every use of `productSelect` after the route-level change, not by a failing test — writing the STAFF-creates-with-price test afterward confirmed the fix, but the reasoning came first, matching CLAUDE.md's "explain before writing code" rule for anything touching pricing.

### Why STAFF could briefly set costPrice/sellingPrice directly at creation without the PIN gate — and why that was reversed one task later
**Superseded — see the entry below.** `04_API_SPEC.md` originally said creating a Product never requires the PIN gate "regardless of role," which read as a deliberate creation-vs-editing distinction and was flagged as such at the time, not silently implemented. The very next task reversed it: the spec was updated so the real rule is "does this request set a real price," never "creating vs. editing" — full OWNER+PIN is required at creation too, the instant a price field is present. Left this entry in place rather than deleting it, since it's a real example of something correctly flagged as a live policy question that then genuinely got resolved differently — the log should show that trail, not erase the version that turned out to be temporary.

### Why POST's price gate needed its own conditional-role-AND-conditional-PIN middleware, distinct from PATCH's
PATCH already had a pattern for this — `requirePinForPriceEdits` wraps `requirePin` conditionally on price fields being present. It couldn't be reused as-is for POST, because PATCH and POST differ in *which* check is conditional: PATCH requires OWNER unconditionally (any edit at all, priced or not) and only the PIN is gated on price; POST allows any role through *unconditionally* when there's no price, meaning both the role check AND the PIN check must be conditional together. `requireOwnerPinForPriceFields` chains `requireRole('OWNER')` manually rather than mounting it as a plain route-level middleware, passing it a callback (`() => requirePin(...)`) as its own `next` — so a failed role check (which already sent its own 403 response) simply never reaches the PIN check, and a passed role check flows straight into it. Same "either/or is unacceptable, must chain" reasoning already established for PATCH's requireRole+requirePin combination, just wired conditionally instead of unconditionally at the route level.

### Why isActive is checked in requireAuth on every request, not just at login
A JWT in this app is stateless and valid for 7 days (`authController.js`'s `TOKEN_EXPIRY`) — there's no server-side session record to delete, no way to "log someone out" from the server side at all, by design (that tradeoff was made deliberately when JWT was chosen over sessions, see the earlier auth-strategy decision). That means a login-time-only `isActive` check would be nearly useless for its actual purpose: deactivating a fired staff member would do nothing until their existing token happened to expire on its own, up to a week later. Checking on every `requireAuth` call closes that gap — `requireAuth` already re-fetches the full user row every request (to re-derive `role`, never trusting the JWT payload), so checking `isActive` on that same already-fetched row costs nothing extra and makes deactivation take effect on literally the next request the deactivated user makes.

### Why login's isActive check runs AFTER password verification, not before
Checking `isActive` before verifying the password would let a request that doesn't even know the correct password learn something real: whether "deactivated account" and "wrong password" produce different responses. That's a new enumeration channel layered on top of the one `login()` already deliberately closes (same message for "no such user" and "wrong password"). Checking after password verification means only someone who has already proven they know the correct credentials gets told the more specific reason — which is also better for a legitimate deactivated staff member, who gets an honest, actionable message instead of being told their real password is "invalid."

### Why the PIN-lockout logic was extracted into a shared verifyPin function instead of copy-pasted into the new self-service PIN endpoint
`PATCH /api/users/me/pin`'s `currentPin` check needs the exact same brute-force protection as the existing price-edit PIN gate (`requirePin.js`) — same secret (`priceEditPinHash`), same 5-attempts/15-minute-lockout policy, same underlying risk. Two separate implementations of "verify a PIN against the lockout state" would inevitably drift eventually — e.g. a future change to `MAX_ATTEMPTS` landing in only one of them, silently leaving the other endpoint's lockout weaker without anyone deciding that on purpose. `verifyPin(userId, suppliedPin)` returns a plain result object rather than sending an HTTP response itself, which is what let it serve two genuinely different callers: `requirePin` (Express middleware, calls `next()` or `sendError`) and `updateOwnPin` (a controller that, on success, still has more of its own work to do — hash and save the new PIN — rather than just letting a request through).

### Why the primary-owner check happens inside createUser using req.user.isPrimaryOwner, not a separate middleware
Every other role restriction in this app is coarse enough to live in route-level middleware (`requireRole('OWNER')` blocks the whole endpoint for non-owners). This restriction is finer-grained: an OWNER can always create a STAFF account, and can only sometimes create another OWNER account, depending on a property of the requester (`isPrimaryOwner`) crossed with a property of the request body (`role === 'OWNER'`). That's not "is this role allowed to hit this route" (middleware's job) — it's "is this specific combination of who's asking and what they're asking for allowed" (the handler's job, since only the handler has parsed the body). `req.user.isPrimaryOwner` is available for this because `auth.js` already re-fetches it fresh from the DB on every request, the same trust model as `role` itself.

### Why the self-deactivation guard is checked before the last-active-owner guard, and why that ordering means one of them is provably unreachable via the live API
Under this route's own auth model — `requireRole('OWNER')` at the route, plus `requireAuth` already rejecting any inactive user's token before a request reaches a handler at all — whoever is making a `deactivateUser` request is, by construction, a currently-active OWNER. Follow that through: if the *target* of a deactivation is the sole remaining active owner, the *requester* (who must also be an active owner to have reached this route in the first place) is necessarily that exact same person. There is no way, via one live HTTP request, for someone else to be the one attempting to deactivate "the last active owner" — they'd have to not exist as an active owner themselves to make the count reach exactly one, which contradicts them being able to make the request at all. This means the last-active-owner guard can only ever fire as a *side effect* of a self-deactivation attempt in every real, reachable, single-request scenario — self-check-first isn't just a style choice, it's the ordering that reflects which guard is actually doing the work in practice. Verified this claim directly rather than asserting it: the "sole owner attempts self-deactivation" test confirms the response is `CANNOT_DEACTIVATE_SELF`, not `LAST_ACTIVE_OWNER` — proof the reasoning holds, not just plausible-sounding.

### Why the last-active-owner guard was still implemented and tested as its own thing, even though it's unreachable standalone via the live API
Two reasons it's not dead weight. First, the spec asks for it explicitly as an independent guard, and "the requester happens to always be the target under the current code" is a fact about *this version* of the code, not a law — if guard 1 were ever refactored, reordered, or a new route added that could reach `deactivateUser` under different conditions, guard 2 is what would still hold the actual invariant the spec cares about (never zero active owners), rather than relying on a coincidence of ordering. Second, and more concretely: a straightforward count-based guard like this is exactly the shape of check that's vulnerable to a TOCTOU race if two *different* owners simultaneously deactivate two *different* owners — a scenario where guard 1 genuinely doesn't apply to either request, and guard 2's count check is the only thing standing in the way (flagged as a known, deliberately-unaddressed gap, since closing it properly needs row-level locking, a different pattern than anything else in this codebase uses). Given guard 2 can't be reached through a normal request, it was verified with a **direct unit test** instead — importing the controller function and calling it with a mocked `req`/`res` where `req.user.id` is deliberately set to something other than the target's id (impossible via the real API, but exactly what isolates guard 2's own branch from guard 1's short-circuit) — the same mocked-middleware technique already used elsewhere in this project for testing `requireRole.js`/`requirePin.js` without a running server.

### Why login()'s response was fixed to include isPrimaryOwner/hasPinSet before building anything else
Discovered while planning the Manage Users screen, not while debugging: `authController.js`'s `login` hardcoded its response shape (`{ id, username, name, role }`) separately from `auth.js`'s `requireAuth`, which had already grown to attach `isPrimaryOwner` (and, later in this same task, `hasPinSet`) to `req.user` for `GET /api/auth/me`. The two were never rewritten together when `auth.js` changed, so a user who had just logged in (via `login`) would have `isPrimaryOwner: undefined` in their client-side auth state, while the exact same user after a page refresh (which calls `/me`) would correctly have `isPrimaryOwner: true`. This is exactly the kind of bug that hides well: everything still "worked" in the sense that nothing threw, a refresh always fixed it, and neither path was individually wrong — they'd just quietly drifted apart. Fixed by making both paths build the user object from the same explicit field list, and flagged before writing the two new screens rather than after, since the Manage Users role dropdown and the SetPin banner both depend on exactly these two fields being present immediately after login, not just after a refresh.

### Why hasPinSet is a derived boolean, computed at the point of exposure, never priceEditPinHash itself
The client genuinely needs to know "does this account have a PIN yet" to decide whether to show the setup prompt — but it must never receive anything that could be used to verify a PIN client-side, or that reveals more than the yes/no fact itself. `!!user.priceEditPinHash` collapses "a bcrypt hash string" down to "true," discarding everything else about the hash before it ever leaves the server — the same "select, don't strip" instinct applied to a computed field instead of a raw column: there was never a version of the response object that had priceEditPinHash on it and then had it removed.

### Why the Manage Users screen proactively disables actions the backend would reject anyway, instead of just letting them fail
Both userController.js guards (self-deactivation, last-active-owner) return real, correct 403s if actually reached via the API — nothing here is about closing a security gap, since the backend enforces both regardless of what the frontend shows. But a button that's guaranteed to fail every time it's clicked is a worse experience than not offering it: the affected row shows a plain-text explanation ("You" / "Last active owner") instead of a doomed control, computed client-side from data already in hand (the fetched user list) rather than an extra request. Structurally, this mirrors the exact backend finding from the previous task: since the viewer of this screen is always themselves a currently-active OWNER, "Last active owner" can in practice only ever describe the viewer's own row — which already shows "You" first, per the same ordering reasoning. It's kept as its own explicit branch anyway, for the same reason the backend kept guard 2 as its own check: correct-by-construction now, not merely correct by coincidence of who happens to be looking.

### Why a freshly-created article's colors come from the global Color list, and each picked color creates its Bundle on the spot
`GET /api/products/:id/valid-colors` — the data source the existing (Matched-article) color-staging flow was built on — only returns colors that already have a `Bundle` for that Product. A brand-new article has zero Bundles by construction, so reusing that endpoint unchanged would mean the color dropdown stayed permanently empty and the whole staging flow the task asked to reuse would be unreachable. The fix isn't a workaround around that endpoint's behavior — it's recognizing that the endpoint's premise (this article's valid colors are already established) simply isn't true yet for a new article, and that receiving stock in a color for the first time IS the moment that becomes true. So a new article's color picker draws from the unfiltered global list, and picking a color for the first time creates its `Bundle` immediately (`POST /api/bundles`) rather than assuming one already exists.

### Why Bundle creation happens eagerly on first pick, not batched at "Add to receipt"
Deferring all Bundle creation to the moment "Add to receipt" is clicked (creating Bundles for every staged color in one batch) would avoid ever creating a Bundle for a color the staff picked and then reconsidered — but it would mean turning `handleAddToReceipt`, a function that's synchronous everywhere else in this screen, into something that awaits N network calls before it can finish, with its own partial-failure handling if call 3 of 5 fails. Creating the Bundle at the moment each color is FIRST selected keeps every other part of the already-built staging interaction (switch-color finalizes the previous pick, "Add to receipt" folds in the still-active one) exactly as synchronous as it already was — only the very first pick of a never-before-seen color gets an async step in front of it, and every subsequent interaction with that color (staging it, abandoning it, re-selecting it) is instant, reading from `resolvedColors` exactly like the Matched branch already did. The tradeoff, accepted deliberately: a color that's picked and then abandoned (switched away from with 0 sets, never staged) leaves behind a real, harmless Bundle no Stock or Transaction ever references — a true fact ("this article legitimately comes in this color") that just happens not to have been used yet this session, not a data-integrity problem.

### Why re-selecting an abandoned color must never re-POST its Bundle
Once a color has been picked for a new article (Bundle created, added to `resolvedColors`), the color dropdown does NOT permanently remove it from the list unless it's actually staged — an abandoned pick (0 sets) stays selectable, since nothing was ever committed for it. Re-picking it is a completely normal, expected flow (staff changing their mind about which color to enter sets for first). `handleColorChange` checks `resolvedColors` — not `stagedColors` — before deciding whether to create a Bundle at all, so a color that's already been resolved (staged OR merely abandoned-but-picked-once) skips the network call entirely and behaves exactly like the Matched branch's instant color-switch. Without this check, re-selecting an abandoned color would attempt a second `POST /api/bundles` for the same `(productId, colorId)` pair, which the backend's own uniqueness constraint would reject with a 409 — not a crash, but a confusing, entirely avoidable error surfaced to someone who did nothing wrong.

### Why "Damaged on arrival" is per-color state, not per-article
The flag lives on `currentDamaged`/each staged entry's `damaged` field — the exact same shape and lifecycle as `currentSets`/`sets` — rather than as one flag on the article being received. A single article entering stock in three colors can easily have only one of those colors actually arrive damaged (a torn box for Navy, Red and Green both fine); a single article-level flag would force an all-or-nothing choice that doesn't match how damage actually happens during a real delivery. Piggybacking directly on the sets stepper's existing reset points (color switch, "Change", post-commit) meant no new reset logic had to be written at all — `currentDamaged` resets everywhere `currentSets` already did, because damage assessment is scoped to "the color currently being set up," identically to quantity.

### Why piecesPerSet is read from lookup.product.sizes at commit time, not carried forward from the sizing step's own state
The New-article sizing step already computes a live pieces-per-set count (`selectedSizes.length`, driving the "= N pieces per set" readout) — the obvious-looking fix would have been to stash that number somewhere and carry it through to the eventual receiptItems entry. Checking first, rather than assuming: `createProduct`'s response (and every other Product read) is built from `productSelect`, which unconditionally includes `sizes` — meaning `lookup.product.sizes` is already a complete, correct answer to "how many pieces per set does this article have," available at the exact moment `handleAddToReceipt` runs, for a Matched article AND a just-created one alike. Reading it fresh there — `lookup.product.sizes.length` — needed no new plumbing, no new state, and unified what would otherwise have been two different code paths (one reading a carried-forward local variable, one reading the fetched Product) into one. This is exactly the kind of thing the task's own wording flagged as worth checking rather than assuming — and checking turned up a simpler answer than the assumption would have produced.

### Why the receipt table groups by productId instead of trusting one receiptItems entry per article
Nothing prevents a staff member from searching and committing the same article twice in one receiving session (e.g. two separate deliveries of the same shirt arriving at different points in the day, or simply forgetting a color had already been added and going back for it) — each "Add to receipt" click appends its own entry to `receiptItems`, so this produces two separate entries sharing the same `productId`. Rendering one table section per raw entry would show that same article twice, which rule 53's "grouped by article" doesn't actually mean — grouping has to happen at render time, keyed on `productId`, merging every entry's colors into one section regardless of how many separate commits produced them. Verified this is real, not a hypothetical edge case, by testing it directly: committing the same article across two separate lookups and confirming exactly one section renders with every row from both commits present.

Rule 3 is actually about size ranges being fixed per Article, unrelated to confirmation UX — and no rule anywhere in that file mentions double confirmation at all. `07_UI_DESIGN_BRIEF.md` §5.2 only specifies a SINGLE confirm dialog with fixed copy ("Save this receipt? ### Why every line submits sequentially and keeps going after a failure, never stopping early
`POST /api/transactions` is atomic per call (one DB transaction each), but nothing ties multiple calls together — and the task is explicit that nothing should: a line that succeeds must stay real even if a later one fails, ruling out any "undo what succeeded" behavior entirely. Given that, stopping at the first failure would leave every line after it in an ambiguous state — never attempted, but indistinguishable from a real failure to the person looking at the result. Running every line regardless produces a complete, unambiguous picture in one pass: exactly which lines are now real stock and exactly which still need saving, which is what actually lets the result message and the leftover table rows work together to tell a non-technical user what to do next without them having to reason about it themselves.

### Why a failed line stays in the table with its exact original data, instead of just being reported as failed
The alternative — clearing the whole receipt regardless of outcome, or leaving failed lines described only in a message — would force the staff member to either lose real work or manually re-enter exactly what they'd already typed once. Keeping the failed line's row in the table, unchanged, means the recovery path is identical to just using the screen normally: press "Save receipt" again. This only works because each submission line already carries enough identity (`entryId` + `colorId`) to be found again after the fact — succeeded lines are filtered out of `receiptItems`, failed ones are kept exactly as they were, and an article group whose every line succeeded disappears from the table entirely rather than lingering as an empty, confusing section.

### Why the damaged flag rides in Transaction.note instead of being dropped at submission time
`Transaction` has no dedicated field for "damaged on arrival" — adding one was explicitly out of scope for this task ("Do not touch... any backend/Transaction changes"). Silently dropping the flag at the exact moment it becomes a real, permanent record would have been the worse failure mode: the whole point of flagging damage during receiving is to have it on the record afterward, not just visible for the few minutes before "Save receipt" is pressed. `note` is the one field the existing endpoint already exposes for exactly this kind of free-text context, so a damaged line's Transaction gets `note: 'Damaged on arrival'` rather than losing the fact entirely. Verified this survives all the way to a real row, not just assumed it: the test reads the fact back from `GET /api/transactions` after a real submission.

### Why GET /api/stock needed productId added before Live Stock View could be built correctly
The endpoint's response carried `productArticleNo` (a bare string) but nothing identifying which Product that article number actually belonged to. That's fine for screens that already know which Factory they're working within (Receive Stock always has a Factory selected first) — but Live Stock View's whole job is grouping stock BY Factory, with no Factory pre-selected, and article numbers are only unique per Factory, never globally (CLAUDE.md's non-negotiable rule). Matching stock rows back to Products by the bare articleNo string would silently misattribute stock the moment two Factories happened to share an article number — not a hypothetical, but a case the schema was explicitly designed to allow. Flagged this before writing any frontend code, since building the grouping logic on an ambiguous join would have meant either shipping a real correctness bug or discovering it only after the fact. Added `productId` to the response (backend) rather than accepting the ambiguity — confirmed the fix actually closes the gap with a test that deliberately puts the same article number under two different Factories and checks their stock never merges or cross-attributes.

### Why the top-level summary shows only 2 stats (sets, pieces) while each Factory section shows 4
`05_BUSINESS_RULES.md` rule 57 is specific about this asymmetry: "Top-level summary shows total sets and total pieces; each factory section repeats the same summary scoped to that factory (article count, sets, pieces, low-stock count)" — the page-wide summary and the per-factory summary are deliberately different shapes, not the same 4 stats repeated at both levels. Followed this literally rather than the earlier (superseded) §5.5 draft's 3-stat description (Articles/Sets/Low stock) — Round 6 text explicitly revises §5.5 where they conflict, and a dedicated Low Stock List screen (§5.7) already exists for a global low-stock view, which is likely why the top level here doesn't need to repeat it.

### Why Kids sizing needed a separate state variable instead of reusing selectedSizes
Adult sizing's `selectedSizes` is an array specifically because multiple sizes combine into one set, and its whole shape (toggle-to-add/remove, `.length` as the piece count) depends on that. The revised Kids sizing is fundamentally not that: exactly one category is ever chosen, and its piece count is a fixed, looked-up property of the category itself, never a count of anything selected. Trying to force both onto the same `selectedSizes` array would have meant either allowing Kids to accumulate multiple entries (wrong — rule 50 is explicit that only one is ever active) or bolting extra logic onto the adult path to enforce "single-select" only when `isKids` is true, which risks exactly the kind of shared-state coupling that made this task's core instruction — "adult sizing's counting logic stays unchanged" — hard to honor cleanly. A second state variable, `selectedKidsCategory` (a single value, not an array), keeps the two systems structurally independent: adult code paths never need to know Kids exists, and vice versa.

### Why the piece-count lookup had to be fixed in TWO places, not one
The live "= N pieces per set" readout (shown during sizing, before the article exists) and `handleAddToReceipt`'s piecesPerSet calculation (used at commit time, after the article exists and colors are being saved) were both computing pieces from a count — `selectedSizes.length` in one case, `lookup.product.sizes.length` in the other — because they're reading from two different data sources at two different moments (local UI selection state vs. the Product object returned by the API). Updating only the live readout would have left a REAL article-creation flow silently wrong: the UI would show the correct fixed count while sizing, then compute the WRONG pieces (based on a 1-row array) the moment that article's colors were actually committed to a receipt — the more consequential of the two bugs, since it affects what actually gets treated as inventory truth. Caught this by explicitly tracing where piecesPerSet gets used anywhere in the file, not just where the Kids UI renders, and verified the fix with a test that specifically checks the COMMITTED receipt row's pieces value (18, not 3) rather than only the live readout during sizing.

### Why GET /api/factories/:id/payable had to move from any-role to OWNER-only before being built
The spec initially marked this endpoint 🔒 (any role), but its numbers are entirely derived from `costPriceSnapshot` — summed across quantities STAFF already knows (they're the ones logging the STOCK_IN transactions). An open payable figure isn't a raw `costPrice` field, but it's arithmetically reverse-engineerable into one: divide the total by known pieces received and a STAFF member has the cost price, reached through subtraction instead of a direct read. Flagged this before writing any code, since building the endpoint open-by-default and tightening it later would have meant a real, if brief, exposure window. Confirmed via the actual test that a STAFF token gets 403, not just that the code intends to block it.

### Why costPriceSnapshot needed an explicit select on Transaction.create, not just a new column
Prisma's `.create()` returns every scalar field of the created row by default when no `select` is given — `transactionController.js`'s create call had none. The moment `costPriceSnapshot` became a real column, every future `POST /api/transactions` response (STAFF included) would have started including it automatically, with no code change anywhere actually asking for that — the exact "select, don't strip" gap this project has caught before, just triggered by adding a column instead of adding a role. Fixed by giving the create call the same explicit, unconditional select every other sensitive-field endpoint already uses — costPriceSnapshot is never returned by this endpoint at all, not even to OWNER, since 04_API_SPEC.md's documented response shape never asked for it either; it exists purely for the payable aggregation to read directly out of the database.

### Why the Kids piece-count lookup had to be duplicated on the backend, not just trusted from the frontend
The payable calculation sums `qtySets × piecesPerSet × costPriceSnapshot` server-side, over real Transaction rows — it has no access to whatever the frontend computed at commit time, only to what's actually stored (one ProductSize row per Kids article, holding the category label). Reusing `sizes.length` uniformly (as adult articles correctly do) would have silently undercounted every Kids contribution to exactly 1/6th, 1/5th, or 1/4th of its real value — the identical class of bug fixed in the frontend receipt table two tasks ago, just now on the money-owed calculation instead of a piece-count display. Verified this specifically with its own test case (a Kids article, its real fixed piece count reflected correctly in the payable sum), not assumed correct by analogy to the already-fixed frontend.

### Why POST /api/transactions rejects DEFECT_RETURN and PARTY_RETURN even though the enum declares them
The Round 11 schema adds both values to the `TransactionType` enum, so the obvious move was to widen the endpoint's `VALID_TYPES` to match the enum exactly — they're the same concept, after all. But the enum and the endpoint answer genuinely different questions. The enum answers "what values can a row in this column legally hold," and both need to be there now so a later phase's rows are insertable without another enum migration (which, per the Concepts entry, is the awkward kind). The endpoint answers "what movements can this API correctly apply to real inventory right now," and neither has designed stock-movement semantics yet — a `PARTY_RETURN` has to add stock back and link a `PartyStockReturn` record; a `DEFECT_RETURN` only touches inventory for BULK severity (rule 40). Accepting them today would mean guessing, and the failure mode of guessing wrong is silently applying the wrong movement to real stock, which is the exact class of error every other rule in this project is arranged to prevent. A `400` saying "not supported yet" is recoverable; a wrong quantity written into inventory is not, because nothing downstream would flag it.

### Why sample tracking's removal touched the stock read endpoint, not just the transaction one
Rule 84 removes `qtyReservedForSample` and the two SAMPLE transaction types. The visible work is in `transactionController.js`, which had real branching logic for both types. But `qtyReservedForSample` was also being explicitly selected and returned by `GET /api/stock` — and once the column is dropped, that select doesn't degrade gracefully, it makes every single call to the endpoint fail outright at the database level (`column "qtyReservedForSample" does not exist`), taking the entire Live Stock View down with it. This is worth noting because a "remove a feature" task naturally focuses on the code that *implements* the feature, while the breakage risk concentrates in the code that merely *reads* it — and reads are scattered wider. The check that actually caught it was a plain grep for the field name across the whole of `backend/src` and `frontend/src` before changing anything, which also turned up a now-inaccurate response-shape comment in `frontend/src/api/stock.js` and three stale references in `04_API_SPEC.md`. Grepping for the removed identifier — rather than reasoning about which files "should" be involved — is what made the blast radius factual instead of assumed.

### Why the color-loading gate is phrased positively ("has settled") rather than negatively ("is not loading")
The minimal patch for the pre-fetch gap would have been `useState(true)` on the loading boolean — one character-ish change, and the first render would say "Loading…" correctly. It was rejected because it fixes the symptom at exactly one point in the lifecycle. The screen doesn't just load once: `resetLookup()` clears everything between articles, and a boolean initialised `true` is only `true` at mount, so the second article looked up in the same session would hit the identical hole again. The three-state version fixes it everywhere by construction, because "nothing has been asked about this article yet" has a name (`idle`) that `resetLookup` can return to.

The gate also had to be written as `colorListsSettled` (all required lists are `'loaded'`) rather than `!anythingLoading`, and those are not equivalent — they differ precisely at the `idle` moments, since an un-started fetch has nothing in flight either. Phrasing it negatively would have silently reintroduced the bug while looking like it had been fixed. Which lists count depends on the source: in bootstrap mode the picker is populated from `globalColors`, so `resolvedColors` having settled says nothing useful on its own. That detail matters because `colorBootstrapMode` flips true in the same batch that `resolvedColors` settles — one render *before* the `globalColors` request even starts — which is why the old code briefly claimed "No colors exist in the system yet" while a color did exist. The measurement caught that second, previously unnoticed instance in 10/10 bootstrap runs, and the same gate closes both.

### Why POST /api/products defaults an omitted categoryId to "Others" instead of requiring it
Round 12 made `Product.categoryId` a required foreign key, but the task was explicitly scoped to schema-and-migration only — no category-picker UI, no `GET /api/categories` endpoint. Left alone, that combination is a contradiction: the schema demands a value the frontend has no way to supply, so `createProduct` (the same endpoint Receive Stock's "Create article" flow already calls) would 500 on every single article creation, discovered empirically by running the exact request the existing frontend already sends and watching it crash with a raw Prisma error. Because "patch it to not crash" and "leave it broken until a follow-up task" are genuinely different, consequential outcomes — one keeps a currently-working, 52-check-tested feature alive, the other breaks it in a way only a future task fixes — this was raised as an explicit question rather than decided unilaterally, unlike the backfill-to-"Others" call for the two pre-existing rows (a one-time historical default, lower stakes than a decision that governs every future create).

The chosen shape matters: `categoryId` stays *optional* in the request, honored as-is when a real one is sent, defaulting to "Others" only when absent — not forced to "Others" unconditionally, and not validated-then-rejected when missing. This keeps today's untouched frontend working exactly as before while leaving the door open for a real category picker to start sending genuine values later with zero further backend change. The default is looked up by name (`findFirst({ where: { name: 'Others' } })`) each time, not a hardcoded id, because nothing about a growing, editable list (`Category.isActive`, rename support) guarantees a fixed id stays valid indefinitely — the same reasoning already applied to Color/Location/Factory lookups elsewhere in this codebase.

### Doc drift, again: 03_DATABASE_SCHEMA.md's Round 12 revision was missing two already-shipped Round 11 fixes
For the second consecutive schema-revision task, the doc's incoming version turned out to be missing state that was live, migrated, and tested in the real database — this time `ProductSize.@@unique([productId, sizeLabel])` and `User.partyStockReturns` were both absent again (the exact same two things the Round 11 task had to restore after the same thing happened then), and the `@@check` comment had reverted to its pre-verification wording. Rather than silently re-fixing this a second time with no comment (which would make it look like nothing had happened, when the same drift recurring twice is itself worth noticing), it's now flagged explicitly in both the response to the user and inline in schema.prisma's own comments, and the live constraints were kept rather than deferring to the doc. The general lesson: when a source-of-truth doc gets revised by an external process between tasks, diffing it against the CURRENTLY LIVE schema (not just reading the new doc in isolation) is what catches silent regressions in the doc itself — treating the doc as unconditionally authoritative would have reintroduced a real bug (the duplicate-size pieces-per-set inflation) a second time.

### Why the Kids-toggle default re-applies on every OFF→ON transition, instead of only the first time
The instruction was "smart-default the picker to Kids without locking it — still changeable," which leaves open exactly *when* the default fires: only the very first time the toggle is switched on, or every time. Chose every time — the check is `if (!isKids)` (i.e. "about to turn on") at the moment of the click, not a one-shot flag that gets consumed and never fires again. The reasoning: each OFF→ON flip is the person actively saying "this is a kids garment" right now, which is exactly the moment "Kids" is the best guess again, regardless of whether they'd previously wandered off to a different category earlier in the same form. The OFF direction deliberately does the opposite — it never touches the category, even though it could "revert" to nothing. Clearing it on OFF would mean a manual pick (e.g. "Hoodie", made *because* the person decided that was more accurate than "Kids") gets silently destroyed by an unrelated toggle flip, which is a real value the app has no business discarding. This asymmetry — re-apply going one direction, never touch going the other — is what makes the field a genuine *default* rather than a rule that fights the user: it offers a guess exactly when a guess is newly relevant, and never overwrites an actual decision.

### Why the two lines that turned out to be genuine bugs in the new test were worth chasing down to the exact character, not worked around
The first hang (two clicks with nothing awaited between them) and the second (a `"pieces"` vs `"piece"` string mismatch) could both have been "fixed" by inserting a longer blind sleep or a looser substring check that happened to match both plurals. Neither shortcut was taken, for the same reason: this project's tests exist to catch real regressions, and a test whose passing condition has been loosened until it stops noticing the exact thing it was checking is worse than no test — it reports green while proving nothing about the singular-vs-plural rendering, or about whether a genuinely stale click ever gets sent. The fix in both cases was to make the assertion match reality exactly (the real rendered string, the real point where a render is guaranteed to have committed), not to make reality's mismatch stop mattering to the assertion.

### Why deactivate/reactivate is gated per-entity by CREATION rules, not by each entity's own PATCH rules or a single blanket policy
Factory and Product both already have an existing PATCH `/:id` endpoint for editing fields (GST/contact, category/price), and both of those are OWNER-only. It would have been easy to make deactivate/reactivate OWNER-only too, by analogy to "editing a record" in general. That would have been wrong: the instruction was specifically to match each entity's *creation* gating, and Factory/Product/Color all let any authenticated role create one — staff add factories and articles constantly as part of normal Receive Stock work. Deactivation is a genuinely different kind of action from "edit this GST number," much closer in spirit to "un-create it" — the same class of action as the creation it reverses, not the same class as field-level editing. Location breaks the pattern the other direction: its own creation is already OWNER-only (unlike the other three), so its deactivate/reactivate stayed OWNER-only too, for the same "match creation" reasoning, not because deactivation is inherently more sensitive for Location specifically.

Party had no precedent to match at all — no create/list endpoint exists yet, so there was nothing to mirror. Rather than default to "any role" just because most of the other four landed there, this was raised as an explicit question, since it's a real security-relevant judgment call with no established answer in the codebase, precisely the kind of ambiguity CLAUDE.md says to ask about rather than assume. Landed on OWNER-only, reasoning that a Party is a customer/shop relationship record — closer to Location (a bounded, deliberately-managed list) than to Color (a low-stakes lookup that grows freely with daily use).

### Why the lockout guards from deactivateUser don't appear anywhere in these five
`deactivateUser` has two guards: never let someone deactivate themselves, and never let the last active OWNER be deactivated. Both exist because of one specific structural fact — the API's own OWNER-only routes require *at least one active OWNER to exist*, or nothing gated behind `requireRole('OWNER')` could ever be reached again, including the reactivate endpoint that would undo the mistake. That's a genuine irreversible-lockout risk unique to User/OWNER. None of Factory/Product/Color/Location/Party have anything analogous — deactivating every single Factory in the system doesn't prevent anyone from creating a new one or reactivating an old one, because `POST /api/factories` isn't gated behind "a Factory must already be active." The guards are protecting against a specific self-referential trap (the permission needed to reverse the action requires the very state the action is destroying), not deactivation in general — so porting them to entities without that trap would have been solving a problem that doesn't exist there, not extra safety.

### Why the Transfer screen selects a Stock ROW, instead of copying Receive Stock's article-then-colour cascade
Receive Stock's selection is two dependent dropdowns: look up an article (creating it if absent), then pick a colour (creating it if absent), scoped to a Factory. Copying that shape onto Transfer would have been the path of least resistance — the components already exist and the screens superficially rhyme ("pick an article and a colour and a quantity"). It would also have been wrong, because the two screens answer different questions.

Whether an article+colour can be *received* depends on almost nothing — that's the point, Receive Stock brings things into existence. Whether it can be *transferred* depends on four facts simultaneously: the Product exists, the Colour exists, a Bundle links them, AND the source Location currently holds `qtySets > 0` of it. A cascade evaluates those one at a time, so it can lead someone through two valid-looking steps into a combination with nothing to move — article 6056 is real, Navy is real, Delhi holds zero Navy. The dead end is only discoverable at the end, after the user has already committed to a path.

Collapsing it to a single flat list of concrete in-stock rows (article + colour + available quantity, filtered to the chosen source) makes that unrepresentable: every visible option is transferable by construction, because the list is built from Stock rows rather than from Products. It's also one decision instead of two, and it carries the available quantity along for free — which is what caps the stepper, so the `INSUFFICIENT_STOCK` error becomes a genuine race-condition backstop rather than something the UI regularly walks people into. This is the same "make the bad state unrepresentable rather than merely unlikely" reasoning behind the three-state colour-loading fix, applied to selection instead of loading.

The deliberate absence of any "+ Create new" affordance is load-bearing, not an omission: a Transfer that could create an article would be a Transfer that moves stock which never existed. That's commented in the file itself precisely because the *lack* of something is invisible to a future reader, who might otherwise "fix" the inconsistency by pattern-matching Receive Stock's `CreatableSelect`.

### Why POST /api/transactions still rejects TRANSFER_OUT/TRANSFER_IN even now that transfers are fully built
The two enum values had been rejected by that endpoint on the grounds that their stock-movement semantics weren't designed yet — a temporary state, same as `DEFECT_RETURN`/`PARTY_RETURN` still are. Now that transfers work, the obvious tidy-up would be to add them to `VALID_TYPES`. That would quietly destroy the invariant the whole feature rests on: a transfer is only ever coherent as a *pair* (out at the source, in at the destination), created together atomically. `POST /api/transactions` creates exactly one Transaction per call, so accepting a lone `TRANSFER_OUT` there would let stock leave Delhi and arrive nowhere — inventory silently vanishing, with a perfectly valid-looking audit row explaining where it went. So the rejection stays, permanently, and the comment now says *why it's permanent* rather than "not built yet" — otherwise the next person to read it would reasonably assume it's stale and remove it. The general shape: when an operation's correctness depends on two writes happening together, the API needs exactly one door for it, and every other door needs to stay locked on purpose.

### Why the Party.name uniqueness race was accepted rather than closed with a migration

### Why POST /api/factory-payments went from role-only to PIN-gated mid-task, reversing an earlier explicit decision
`POST /api/factory-payments` originally shipped OWNER-role-only, no PIN — the controller's own comment said so explicitly, and `04_API_SPEC.md` documented it that way, with real reasoning: recording a payment isn't itself a cost/selling-price read or write, so it didn't get the same gate as `PATCH /api/products/:id`. ### Why Transfer's staged batching is one flat list, not Receive Stock's two-tier stagedColors → receiptItems structure
Receive Stock needs two tiers because a single commit unit (one article) can legitimately contain several colors, so colors have to be staged *within* an article before the article itself gets added to the receipt. A Transfer line has no such grouping need — one Stock row + one quantity + one destination is already a complete, self-sufficient thing to move, and requirement 2 explicitly asked for staged lines to grow across different articles, not to be committed one article-group at a time. Copying Receive Stock's two-tier shape here would have added a grouping step with nothing real for it to group, so `stagedLines` is a single flat array instead, and `finalizeActiveLine`/`handleStageAnother` mirror Receive Stock's `finalizeActiveColor`/`handleStageAnother` mechanism (fold the active selection into the staged array, make "stage this and let me pick another" an explicit, visible button rather than a side effect of just clicking a different row) without mirroring the two-tier structure the mechanism happened to sit inside there.

### Why a staged line's cap accounts for what's already staged against the same row, not just the last-fetched qtySets
Requirement 6 asked for each staged line's quantity to be capped by "that row's real available qtySets." Read literally against only the raw, last-fetched `row.qtySets`, that cap would be fictional the moment the same row gets staged more than once in one batch: a 5-available row staged for 5 sets still *shows* 5 available (nothing has actually been submitted yet), so a second staging pass against the same row could push the batch to a locally-staged total of 10 against 5 truly on hand. `remainingForRow()` subtracts what's already staged for that `bundleId` from the raw figure, so the stepper's max — and the number shown in the picker itself — reflects what's really still stageable, not a number that's already gone stale the moment the first line for that row exists. This is a UI-level convenience only; the backend's guarded UPDATE inside `POST /api/transfers` remains the actual enforcement, exactly as the pre-existing per-row cap comment already said before batching existed.

### Why each staged Transfer line snapshots its own destination instead of reading `toLocationId` live at submit time
Only a source-Location change resets the staged list (requirement 5); nothing resets it when the destination dropdown changes, so it's possible — not the common case, but possible — to stage a line while "To" is Gurgaon, then change "To" to Mumbai before staging a second line. Reading `toLocationId` live at submit time would silently redirect the first line to Mumbai too, changing where already-staged stock is headed without the person ever having decided that. Each staged line snapshots `toLocationId`/`toLocationName` the moment it's finalized instead — the same snapshot-not-live-read principle Receive Stock's rule 52 already established for Factory/Location/price — and the staged list shows each line's own destination explicitly (`→ {toLocationName}`) so a genuinely mixed-destination batch is visible rather than hidden behind an assumed single "to."

### Why the in-flight-submission race was fixed with both a UI disable AND a merge-not-replace safeguard, not just one
Found while tracing an unrelated edge case (the fully-staged-row re-select question): nothing stopped a user from staging a new line while a previous batch was still submitting, because `canStage` never checked `submitting` and the stock-row buttons had no `disabled` prop at all. The real damage wasn't that the new line could be double-submitted — it was silent data loss: `handleConfirmedSubmit` snapshots `stagedLines` into a local `lines` var at the very start, then at the end does `setStagedLines(failedLines)`, which is a flat *replace*. Anything staged into `stagedLines` after the snapshot was taken but before that final `setStagedLines` call would simply never appear in `failedLines` (it was never part of `lines`, so it was never submitted, never failed, never retried) and would vanish the instant the replace landed — no error, no trace, nothing in the outcome banner.

Fixed both layers deliberately, not just the more visible one. Disabling the stock-row buttons and "+ Add to transfer" (`canStage` now includes `!submitting`) closes the gap for every real user going through the actual UI — that's the primary fix, and it's what the empirical test exercises with a genuinely held-open `POST` promise (not a fixed timeout) so there's a real window to attempt the blocked interaction during. The merge fix — computing `submittedIds` from the original `lines` snapshot, filtering current `stagedLines` down to whatever *isn't* in that set, and only then prepending `failedLines` — is deliberately kept as a second, independent layer rather than treated as redundant once the UI is disabled: a `disabled` prop is a rendering-layer guarantee, not a data-layer one, and the two have different failure modes (a future edit could easily drop `!submitting` from `canStage` without touching `handleConfirmedSubmit` at all, silently reopening the exact same hole). The merge logic was verified as a pure computation against both outcomes (submitted line succeeds vs. fails) rather than only through the UI test, specifically because the scenario it protects against — the UI block being bypassed — is by definition not reachable through the UI test that proves the first layer works.

### `@@check(...)` was specified in the schema doc, but no such thing exists in Prisma
**(1)** Round 11 of `03_DATABASE_SCHEMA.md` specified `@@check(qtySets >= 0, name: "stock_qty_sets_non_negative")` on `Stock`, with a note to verify it compiles before relying on it. It looked entirely plausible — it sits alongside `@@unique` and `@@index`, follows their exact naming convention, and CHECK constraints are a completely standard Postgres feature, so a schema language that already exposes unique constraints and indexes exposing check constraints too is a reasonable expectation. **(2)** `prisma validate` rejected it: `P1012 — Error validating: This line is not a valid field or attribute definition`. **(3)** Two details in that error message distinguish "this version doesn't support it yet" from "this has never existed." The error is a *parse* error about the line not being a valid definition at all — not a semantic complaint like "unknown attribute `@@check`" or "requires preview feature X," which is what Prisma emits for a real-but-unavailable feature. So the parser doesn't recognise the token whatsoever; there's no preview flag that would turn it on. Isolated it in a throwaway two-field schema first, so nothing else in the real schema could be confounding the result. **(4)** Wrote the constraint as raw SQL appended to the migration file instead: `ALTER TABLE "Stock" ADD CONSTRAINT "stock_qty_sets_non_negative" CHECK ("qtySets" >= 0);`, and replaced the `@@check` line in both `schema.prisma` and the schema doc with a comment explaining where the constraint actually lives and why it can't be declared inline. **(5)** This is the correct fix rather than dropping the constraint, because the *intent* — a database-level guarantee that stock can never go negative — is fully achievable; only Prisma's declarative syntax for it is missing. The genuine risk with hand-written SQL in a Prisma project is that the migration engine later sees the constraint as "drift" (something in the database that the schema doesn't describe) and generates a migration dropping it. That's not a hypothetical worry, so it was tested rather than assumed: running `prisma migrate diff` against both databases *after* applying returned "This is an empty migration," proving the engine doesn't model CHECK constraints at all and therefore leaves this one permanently alone. The one real cost is invisibility — someone reading `schema.prisma` won't see it — which the comment on the `Stock` model is there to mitigate. And crucially the constraint was verified to actually *work*, not just to exist: a raw SQL `UPDATE` setting `qtySets = -1` (deliberately bypassing every application-layer guard, since that's the only path this constraint exists to catch) was rejected by Postgres naming the constraint, with the row left unchanged.

### Chasing a "race condition" that did not exist, and finding a permanent bug underneath it
**(1)** Two tests failed intermittently (2 of 5 regression sweeps) with the color dropdown reading "All colors staged" while disabled. That state is only reachable when `colorBootstrapMode` is false while the loading flags have settled, so the obvious reading was a race: `setColorBootstrapMode(true)` happens in the fetch's `.then`, `setResolvedColorsLoading(false)` in its `.finally`, and if those two landed in separate renders there'd be a window with bootstrap still false and loading already finished. Intermittent failure plus two state updates in different promise callbacks is a genuinely reasonable case for suspecting an ordering race. **(2)** The hypothesis survived nothing. 60 repeated runs of the exact failing scenario in isolation produced zero failures; sampling the DOM every 25ms never caught the bad state; keeping JSDOM instances alive to amplify contention did nothing. **(3)** What finally worked was abandoning inference and logging the actual ordering — a temporary `RSLOG` recording every state transition and every render's derived values, plus a dump hook on the real test so a failure would print its own timeline. The captured log immediately killed the hypothesis: `.then` and `.finally` flush in the *same* microtask batch, so bootstrap and loading always land in one render — the window being hunted cannot open. The same log showed the real culprit one render earlier: `611.84ms RENDER {lookup:"matched", bootstrap:false, resLoading:false, resN:0, disabled:true}`, then `617.23ms resolvedEffect:START`. The color UI renders the instant the lookup resolves, but `resolvedColorsLoading` starts as `false` and only becomes `true` inside an effect, which runs *after* that render commits. Measuring it across 24 runs: the gap is present in **100%** of lookups, lasting 2-6ms. It was never a race; it is a deterministic bug on every single lookup. The only random part was whether the tests' 100ms poll happened to sample inside it. The dump also corrected a second mistake — the failing check was in step [3] (an article that *has* colors), not step [2] as previously reported, proving the gap wasn't specific to the colorless path. **(4)** Replaced both loading booleans with explicit `'idle' | 'loading' | 'loaded'` statuses and gated the placeholder and `disabled` on `colorListsSettled` (positively: "has everything this article depends on finished") instead of on "nothing is currently in flight." **(5)** This is the correct fix rather than raising the tests' poll timeout or initialising the boolean to `true`, because the defect is a **representation** problem, not a timing one: a boolean can only encode "fetching right now", so "not asked yet" and "asked, answered, empty" collapse into the same `false`, and the UI was forced to guess. Adding the missing state makes the bad render unrepresentable rather than merely unlikely. Initialising the boolean to `true` would have papered over the same hole — it fixes this one screen's first render while leaving "finished and empty" still indistinguishable from "never started" for every later reset. Loosening the tests would have been worse still: they were correctly reporting a real defect a user can see.

### Proving a fix by measurement, not just by the suite turning green
**(1)** After the three-state fix the full sweep passed 508/508 with no test file modified, which is the normal bar for "done" in this project. **(2)** That bar was not sufficient here. The bug had a ~4% chance of being observed per lookup, so a green suite is exactly what a *broken* build looks like most of the time — the suite had passed 3 of 5 sweeps while the bug was present. **(3)** Built a sampler that polls the real shipped bundle (no instrumentation) roughly every millisecond throughout a lookup and records every distinct visible state, then — critically — ran it against a deliberately built **control** bundle whose gate used the old "nothing in flight" semantics with everything else identical. **(4)** The control flagged a premature claim in **20/20** runs; the fixed bundle showed **0/30** across ~562 samples per run, with the sequence collapsing to just `Loading… → correct terminal state`. **(5)** The control is what makes the zero meaningful: without it, "0 bad states" is equally consistent with "the fix works" and "the sampler cannot see a 4ms window", and those are indistinguishable from the output alone. ### A waitFor that was already satisfied by the PREVIOUS state, so it asserted against stale data
**(1)** The last check of the new Transfer screen test switches the source location from A to B and confirms the list re-scopes to B's stock. The `||` made it worse by giving two ways to pass early instead of one. **(4)** Rewrote the predicate to wait for the *new* data specifically — `rowTexts(doc).some((t) => t.includes('XferRed…'))` — and added a second assertion that A's signature row is *gone*, so the check now proves the list re-scoped rather than merely that it contains something. **(5)** This is the correct fix because the original predicate was testing "is there anything here," which was true before, during, and after the thing being waited for — it had no power to distinguish the states it was supposed to separate. The general rule this makes concrete, and which the earlier `'Loading…'`-poll bug was a different face of: a `waitFor` condition must be **false in the starting state**, or it isn't a wait at all, it's a no-op that happens to be followed by an assertion. When waiting for a transition from one populated state to another populated state, key the predicate on something unique to the destination state, never on a generic "is it populated yet" that the origin state already satisfies.

## Concepts

**ORM (Object-Relational Mapper)** — Prisma, in our case. Instead of writing raw SQL by hand to talk to the database, you describe your data as JavaScript-like objects and the ORM translates that into SQL for you. You still end up understanding the database structure (the schema file basically *is* your data model), you just don't hand-write every query.

**Migration** — a recorded, versioned change to the database structure (e.g. "add a Product table"). Every time the schema changes, a new migration file gets created so the change can be applied to any copy of the database (your laptop, the live server) in the same order, and so there's a history of how the database evolved over time.

**Middleware** — code that runs *in between* a request arriving at the backend and it actually being handled. Used here for things like "check the user is logged in" or "check the user's role is OWNER" before the real endpoint logic even runs — a gatekeeper step.

**async / await** — most backend code has to wait on something slow (a database query, a network call) before it can continue. `async`/`await` is JavaScript's way of writing "pause here until this finishes" without freezing everything else the server is doing. You'll see it on almost every backend function that touches the database.

**REST API / endpoint** — a REST API is just an agreed set of URLs (endpoints) the frontend can call, each doing one specific thing (e.g. `POST /api/transactions` = "log a stock movement"). "Endpoint" just means one specific URL+action combination.

**Environment variable** — a setting (like the database connection string or a secret key) kept outside the actual code, usually in a `.env` file, so secrets never get committed to version control and the same code can run against different databases (your laptop vs. the live server) without editing code.

**JWT / session** — after you log in, the server needs a way to know it's still you on the next request without asking for your password every time. A session (a reference stored server-side) or a JWT (a signed token stored client-side) are two ways of doing that. We're using one of these — check the current code for which.

**package.json / npm install** — `package.json` is a project's manifest: its name, and every external library (package) it depends on. Running `npm install <package>` downloads that package (and anything *it* depends on) into a local `node_modules/` folder, and records the version in `package.json`'s `dependencies`. Anyone else who clones the project just runs `npm install` with no arguments and gets the exact same set of packages — the code itself doesn't need to be copied, just the manifest.

**dependencies vs. devDependencies** — both live in `package.json`, but mean different things. `dependencies` (installed with plain `npm install <pkg>`) are packages the app needs *at runtime* — e.g. `express`, since the live server actually calls into it. `devDependencies` (installed with `npm install -D <pkg>`) are tools only needed *while building/developing* — e.g. `nodemon`, which restarts the server on file changes but has nothing to do with how the deployed app behaves. Keeping them separate matters because some deployment steps install only `dependencies` to keep the production install smaller.

**CORS (Cross-Origin Resource Sharing)** — by default, a browser blocks JavaScript running on one origin (e.g. the Vite dev server at `localhost:5173`) from making requests to a different origin (e.g. the Express API at `localhost:3001`) — a security default, not a bug. The `cors` middleware on the backend explicitly tells the browser "requests from this specific origin are allowed," via the `Access-Control-Allow-Origin` response header. This only became necessary *because* frontend and backend are two separate projects/deployments (see the Next.js decision above) — a merged framework wouldn't hit this at all, since there'd be no cross-origin request to begin with.

**nodemon** — a dev-only tool that watches your backend source files and automatically restarts the Node process whenever one changes, so you don't have to manually stop/rerun `node src/server.js` after every edit. Only affects the local dev workflow — production runs plain `node src/server.js` (the `start` script), not nodemon.

**Migration (in practice, this project's first one)** — running `npx prisma migrate dev --name init_phase1_inventory` did two things: (1) generated a `migration.sql` file under `backend/src/prisma/migrations/` containing the actual `CREATE TABLE`/`CREATE TYPE` SQL for every Phase 1 model (User, Factory, Product, ProductSize, Color, Bundle, Location, Stock, Transaction), and (2) ran that SQL against the real Neon Postgres database, plus recorded in a `_prisma_migrations` table that this migration has been applied. That migration file is what makes the schema change reproducible — anyone else (or a future deployment) runs the same file against their own database and ends up with identical tables, instead of everyone hand-typing `CREATE TABLE` statements and hoping they match.

**Enum (in Prisma)** — a field restricted to one of a fixed set of named values, enforced by the database itself, not just application code. This schema has two: `Role` (`OWNER`/`STAFF`) and `TransactionType` (`STOCK_IN`/`STOCK_OUT`/`DEFECT_RETURN`/`PARTY_RETURN`). Trying to insert a `User` with `role: "MANAGER"` would fail at the database level, not just get silently accepted — a safety net beneath whatever the application code checks.

Worth knowing: **removing** a value from an enum is much harder than adding one, because Postgres has no `ALTER TYPE ... DROP VALUE`. When the Round 11 revision deleted `SAMPLE_OUT`/`SAMPLE_RETURN`, Prisma had to generate a four-step dance instead — create a brand-new type with only the surviving values, convert the column over to it, drop the old type, then rename the new one into its place. The practical consequence: that conversion only succeeds if **no existing row still holds a value being removed**, because there'd be nothing valid to convert it into. That's exactly why the pre-migration check counted transactions by type first (the answer was zero, so it applied cleanly) rather than running the migration and hoping. An enum is genuinely part of the database's type system, not just a list the application agrees to respect — which is what makes it a real safety net, and also what makes editing it a real migration.

**CHECK constraint** — a rule the database enforces on every single write to a column, rejecting anything that violates it. `Stock` has one: `CHECK ("qtySets" >= 0)`, meaning Postgres itself refuses to store a negative stock quantity, no matter what code attempts it.

This connects to two things already in this log. First, foreign keys and `@@unique` (above) are the same *kind* of thing — guarantees living in the database rather than in application code, so they hold even against a path nobody anticipated. Second, it layers underneath the atomic guarded `updateMany` in `transactionController.js`: that guard (`where: { qtySets: { gte: qtySets } }`) is what actually produces the friendly `INSUFFICIENT_STOCK` error a user sees, and it stops the bad write long before the database is asked to. The CHECK never fires in normal operation. It exists for the paths that skip the controller entirely — someone running raw SQL against the database by hand, or a future controller written without the guard. Belt and braces, where the braces give a good error message and the belt just refuses to let anything through.

One quirk specific to this project: Prisma 6.19.3 has **no way to declare a CHECK constraint in `schema.prisma`** (see the Mistakes & Fixes entry on `@@check`), so this one lives as hand-written SQL inside a migration file. That means reading `schema.prisma` alone will not tell you the constraint exists — hence the explanatory comment parked on the `Stock` model pointing at it.

**Loading state: why a boolean is usually the wrong shape (and what to use instead)** — the reflex when fetching data in a component is a pair like `const [items, setItems] = useState([])` and `const [loading, setLoading] = useState(false)`. It reads naturally, and it's what most tutorials show. It also has a hole in it, which caused a real rendered bug on the Receive Stock screen.

The prerequisite idea is *when* a fetch actually starts. In React, a component first **renders** (produces UI from its current state), that UI is **committed** to the page, and only *then* do its `useEffect` callbacks run. Fetching happens in an effect, so the order is always: render with the starting state → show it → start fetching. There is therefore guaranteed to be at least one moment where the UI is on screen and the request has not been made yet.

Now the hole. A boolean can only answer "am I fetching *right now*". Three genuinely different situations exist:

1. haven't started asking yet — `loading === false`, `items === []`
2. asking, waiting for the answer — `loading === true`
3. asked, got the answer, it was empty — `loading === false`, `items === []`

States 1 and 3 are **identical** in the data, but mean opposite things. The component is forced to guess, and whichever it picks is wrong half the time. Ours guessed "state 3" and rendered `"All colors staged"` on a disabled dropdown — a confident, false claim about an article it had not queried, on 100% of lookups for 2-6ms.

The fix is to stop encoding the wrong thing. Replace the boolean with a value that names all three: `useState('idle')`, moving to `'loading'` when the request starts and `'loaded'` when it settles. Now each situation has its own name, and the two that used to collide are distinct. In [ReceiveStock.jsx](frontend/src/pages/ReceiveStock.jsx) this is `resolvedColorsStatus` / `globalColorsStatus`, and every message that asserts something about which colors exist is gated behind `colorListsSettled` — so before the answer arrives the UI says `"Loading…"`, which is honest, instead of guessing.

Two practical notes. `'loaded'` is set in `.finally`, so it means "the question has been answered" whether the answer was a list, an empty list, or a failure — an error leaves the picker usable and reports itself through its own error banner, rather than stranding it disabled forever. And a reset (`resetLookup()`) must return the status to `'idle'`, not leave it `'loaded'` — otherwise the next article inherits a stale "answer already known" for something never asked about, which is the original confusion wearing a different hat.

This connects to the **async/await** and **useEffect** entries: the gap exists precisely *because* effects run after render, so anything a component says before its first effect is a statement made without data. It generalises well beyond colors — any "no results found" message is making a claim, and is worth checking against whether the search has actually run yet.

**Foreign key / relation** — e.g. `Product.factoryId` plus the `factory Factory @relation(...)` line: this tells Postgres that every `Product.factoryId` value must correspond to a real row in the `Factory` table — you can't create a `Product` pointing at a `Factory` that doesn't exist. Prisma turns each of these into a real foreign-key constraint at the database level, not just a naming convention.

**`@@unique([a, b])` (composite unique constraint)** — a uniqueness rule spanning *two* columns together, rather than one column alone. `Product` uses `@@unique([articleNo, factoryId])`: two different factories can both have an article "A101" (each unique *within* its own factory), but the same factory can't have two Products both named "A101" — enforcing "article numbers unique per Factory, never globally" (CLAUDE.md's non-negotiable rule) directly at the database level, not just in application logic.

**Routes vs. Controllers** — two files usually cooperate to handle one endpoint. The *route* file (e.g. `backend/src/routes/stock.js`) just declares "when a request hits this URL with this HTTP method, call this function" — it's a directory, not logic. The *controller* file (e.g. `backend/src/controllers/stockController.js`, once it exists) holds the actual logic that runs: reading the request, talking to the database via Prisma, deciding what to send back. Splitting them keeps "what URL triggers this" separate from "what actually happens," so a route file stays short and skimmable even as the underlying logic grows. Middleware (see above) sits in front of both — it runs before the route's handler gets control at all.

**JSON-RPC over HTTP (as used by Prisma Studio)** — a pattern where instead of many differently-shaped REST endpoints (`GET /factories`, `POST /products`, ...), a client sends every request to *one* URL (Studio uses `POST /api`) with a JSON body naming which "action" it wants (e.g. `{ action: "clientRequest", payload: { modelName: "Factory", operation: "findMany" } }`), and the server dispatches internally based on that field. Found this while tracing today's Studio investigation — Studio's browser UI talks to its own local Node server this way, which in turn calls the same generated Prisma Client this project's own code uses.

**JWT (JSON Web Token)** — a signed, tamper-proof string the server hands the client after a successful login (`authController.js`'s `login` function does this with `jwt.sign({ userId: user.id }, process.env.JWT_SECRET, { expiresIn: '7d' })`). It has three dot-separated parts: a header, a payload (here just `{ userId, iat, exp }` — deliberately *not* the role, see the decision above), and a signature computed from the first two using `JWT_SECRET`. Anyone can *read* a JWT's payload (it's just base64, not encrypted) — the security comes entirely from the signature: only someone holding `JWT_SECRET` could have produced a signature that verifies, so tampering with the payload (e.g. changing `userId`) invalidates the signature and `jwt.verify()` throws. This is what makes it "stateless" — the server doesn't need to remember anything about the token, just re-verify the signature on each request.

**Bearer token / Authorization header** — the standard way an API client attaches a credential to a request when not using cookies: an HTTP header shaped `Authorization: Bearer <token>`. "Bearer" means exactly what it sounds like — whoever *holds* (bears) the token is treated as authenticated, no further proof needed. This project's `auth.js` middleware reads this header, extracts the token after `Bearer `, and verifies it — if the header is missing or doesn't start with `Bearer `, the request is rejected before even trying to decode anything.

**Upsert** — Prisma's `upsert` means "update if a matching row exists, otherwise create it" (`UPDATE` + `INSERT` = "upsert"), based on a `where` clause that must target a unique field. `seed.js` used this originally, but has since moved to an explicit `findUnique` followed by a conditional `create`/`update` instead — `upsert`'s single `update` object can't express "only touch this one field, and only if it's currently empty," which the PIN-backfill logic needed (see the decision above). Still a useful pattern elsewhere for genuinely "create-or-replace-wholesale" cases.

**Account lockout / rate limiting a sensitive action** — deliberately slowing down or temporarily blocking further attempts after repeated failures at something security-sensitive (here, `requirePin.js`'s 5-strikes-then-15-minute-lock on the price-edit PIN). The core idea: a secret with limited entropy (a 6-digit PIN has "only" a million possibilities, versus a real password) can still be made practically unguessable if an attacker is only allowed a handful of tries before being locked out — the entropy alone isn't enough on its own, the *limit on attempts* is what actually makes brute-forcing infeasible in practice.

**Adding a required (NOT NULL) column to a table that already has rows in it** — a genuinely different, harder problem than adding a required column to an empty table. `Product.name` (see the decision above about it being missing entirely) could be added as required in one step, safely, specifically *because* the `Product` table had zero rows at that moment — there was nothing that could violate the new rule. `Product.categoryId` (Round 12) hit the opposite case: the table already had two real rows, both with no category ever set, and the new rule says every row MUST have one. Two things can't both be true at once — "every row has a category" and "these two rows have none" — so the database will flatly refuse a single `ALTER TABLE ... ADD COLUMN categoryId TEXT NOT NULL` the instant it reaches an existing row with nothing to put there.

The fix is to never ask for both guarantees in the same instant — split it into steps, each one valid on its own:
1. Add the column *without* the NOT NULL restriction (`ADD COLUMN categoryId TEXT`) — every existing row instantly gets `NULL` here, which is allowed.
2. **Backfill**: `UPDATE` every row that has `NULL` to some real, defensible value. Here, all pre-existing product rows had never had a category at all, so there was no real classification to preserve — they were set to "Others" specifically because that's the category whose entire purpose (per its own schema comment) is being the catch-all for exactly this situation, not a fabricated guess about what those products actually are.
3. *Only now*, with zero `NULL`s left in the column, tighten the rule: `ALTER TABLE ... ALTER COLUMN categoryId SET NOT NULL`. This step now succeeds, because by this point it's checking something that's already true.

This is the same idea as validating a form before disabling the "wrong input" state — you can't demand correctness and check for it in the same breath if the data might not already satisfy it; you have to *make it* satisfy it first, then lock the rule in. Prisma's own migration-diff tool doesn't do this sequencing automatically — asked to go straight from "optional string column" to "required relation," it generates the single unsafe `ADD COLUMN ... NOT NULL` statement, which is exactly why this migration was written by hand rather than taken as-generated (see the migration file's own comment for the full three-step SQL).

**Backfilling a schema-level default vs. defaulting it in application code — two different layers, two different failure risks** — a required field can be given a fallback value in either place, and they protect different things. The Round 12 `categoryId` migration's backfill (above) is a *one-time*, historical fix: it only ever touches the two rows that existed at the exact moment the migration ran. It says nothing about the *next* product anyone creates — that request still has to supply a real `categoryId`, or the database rejects it outright, because the column is now `NOT NULL` with no default. Separately, `productController.js`'s `createProduct` was given its own, ongoing fallback (default to "Others" when the request doesn't send one) — this is application code choosing to substitute a value *before* the request ever reaches the database, precisely because no category-picker UI exists yet to supply a real one. If that controller-level fallback were removed, the migration's backfill wouldn't help at all — it already ran, once, in the past; every request after that point needs its own answer. The general shape: a migration's backfill fixes what already exists, at the moment it runs; a controller's default keeps deciding what to do about what doesn't exist yet, on every future request, for as long as the code stays that way.

**Mocking `req`/`res` to test Express middleware without a server** — Express middleware is just a plain function `(req, res, next) => {...}` — nothing about it requires a real HTTP server or network round-trip. `req` and `res` can be substituted with plain JS objects that mimic just the parts the middleware actually uses (e.g. `res.status(code).json(body)` — just two chainable functions), and `next` with a function that records whether it was called. This is how `requireRole.js`/`requirePin.js` got tested here: called directly in a small script against the real seeded database, with no port to bind and nothing that could collide with a leftover process from an earlier session (see the port-collision entry in Mistakes & Fixes above).

**Username enumeration** — a security leak where an app's *error messages* accidentally reveal which usernames exist, e.g. "no such user" vs. "wrong password" as two different messages. An attacker can use that difference alone to build a list of real usernames without ever guessing a correct password, then focus password-guessing effort only on confirmed accounts. `authController.js`'s `login` deliberately returns the exact same "Invalid username or password" message and status code whether the username doesn't exist or the password is wrong, so the response itself gives nothing away.

**Prisma `select`** — controls exactly which columns a query fetches from the database, as opposed to fetching a full row and picking fields out afterward in JS. `productController.js`'s `productSelect(role)` builds this object dynamically, only including `costPrice: true` when `role === 'OWNER'` — for a STAFF request, the generated SQL query itself never asks Postgres for that column. This is different from (and stronger than) fetching everything and doing `delete product.costPrice` before responding: with `select`, there is no `costPrice` property on the resulting JS object at any point, so a later bug that spreads the object (`{ ...product }`) can't accidentally reintroduce the leak — with delete-after-fetch, it always could.

**Foreign key `RESTRICT` (referential integrity on delete)** — when table B has a foreign key pointing at table A (like `ProductSize.productId` → `Product.id`), the database needs a policy for what happens if someone tries to delete the referenced row in A while rows in B still point to it. `RESTRICT` (Prisma's default when a relation doesn't specify `onDelete`) means: refuse the delete outright, error out, change nothing. This is what blocked the test-cleanup script's `prisma.product.deleteMany()` until its child `ProductSize` rows were deleted first — a real database-level guarantee that a `Product` can't vanish out from under its own sizes.

**Prisma error codes (`P2002`, `P2003`, ...)** — when a database operation fails a constraint, Prisma Client throws an error with a specific `code` property identifying *which* kind of constraint failed, rather than a generic error. `P2002` = unique constraint violated (used in `createProduct` to detect a duplicate `articleNo`+`factoryId` and turn it into a clean 409); `P2003` = foreign key constraint violated (used there to detect a `factoryId` that doesn't correspond to a real `Factory` and turn it into a 404). Catching these by code, right where the operation that could trigger them runs, avoids a separate "check first" query that could race against another request doing the same thing at the same moment.

**Middleware chaining / short-circuiting** — an Express route can list more than one middleware function before its final handler (e.g. `router.patch('/:id', requireAuth, requireRole('OWNER'), requirePinForPriceEdits, updateProduct)`); each one runs in order and must call `next()` to let the next one run. If any middleware in the chain sends a response instead of calling `next()` (as `requireRole`/`requirePin` do on failure, via `sendError`), everything after it — including the real route handler — never runs at all. This is what structurally guarantees `04_API_SPEC.md`'s "do not partially apply the update" requirement for `PATCH /api/products/:id`: if the PIN check fails, `updateProduct` is never even called, so there's no code path where a partial write could happen.

**Case-sensitive vs. case-insensitive matching (Postgres/Prisma)** — a plain Postgres `UNIQUE` index (what `name String @unique` in the schema creates) compares text byte-for-byte, so `"Navy"` and `"navy"` are two different values as far as the database is concerned — the constraint only blocks an exact repeat. Prisma exposes case-insensitive comparison via `mode: 'insensitive'` on a string filter (`colorController.js`'s `findFirst({ where: { name: { equals: trimmed, mode: 'insensitive' } } })`), which Prisma translates into a case-folding SQL comparison instead of a plain `=`. This is an application-level check, separate from the database's own constraint — the two aren't the same guarantee, which is why both the pre-check *and* a `P2002` catch exist side by side (see the decision above).

**Race condition / lost-update problem** — happens when two things read the same piece of shared state, both decide what to do based on that read, and both then write — without either one seeing what the other did. Concretely here: two `STOCK_OUT` requests against the same Stock row could both read `qtySets: 5`, both compute "5 - 3 = 2, that's fine," and both write `2` — even though the *correct* answer after two separate `-3` operations should have been `-1` and rejected. The fix isn't "check more carefully in JavaScript" (any read-then-write-later pattern has this same gap, no matter how careful the check) — it's making the check and the write a single, indivisible database operation, which is what `POST /api/transactions`'s WHERE-clause guard does (see the decision above).

**Prisma interactive transactions (`$transaction(async (tx) => {...})`)** — wraps a whole sequence of Prisma calls in one real Postgres transaction (`BEGIN` ... `COMMIT`/`ROLLBACK`), using the special `tx` client passed into the callback instead of the normal `prisma` client. If the callback throws (for any reason — a thrown validation error, a database error, anything), Prisma automatically rolls back every write the callback made, even ones from earlier in the same callback that individually "succeeded" before the throw. This is different from Prisma's other transaction form, the array form (`$transaction([op1, op2])`), which only runs a fixed, pre-decided list of operations together — it can't express "read a value, then decide based on it whether to error out," which `createTransaction` in `transactionController.js` needs to do (find-or-create the Stock row, conditionally reject, only then insert the Transaction record).
Worked example, traced through concretely: if `tx.stock.upsert` and `tx.stock.updateMany` both already ran successfully — the Stock row's new quantities are written, but only as an uncommitted version tagged to this transaction (Postgres's MVCC means no other connection can see it yet, though this same transaction can read its own writes) — and then `tx.transaction.create` throws for any reason, Prisma issues `ROLLBACK`. That discards *everything* since `BEGIN` as one unit: the quantity change reverts, and if `upsert` had just created a brand-new zero-value Stock row (first-ever activity for that bundle/location), that row is gone entirely, not merely reset. The database ends up exactly as if the request had never arrived — there's no partial-commit state to reason about, because Postgres transactions don't have one.

**Express error-handling middleware (the 4-argument signature)** — a normal Express middleware function takes 3 arguments, `(req, res, next)`. An error handler takes 4: `(err, req, res, next)` (`errorHandler.js`). Express doesn't detect this by name or by where it's defined — purely by counting the function's parameters. Write one with only 3 params and Express treats it as ordinary middleware that happens to never get called, since nothing ever routes a normal request to it; the `next` parameter has to stay even though `errorHandler.js` never calls it, just to keep the arity at 4. It also has to be registered with `app.use()` *after* every route it's meant to protect — Express walks the middleware stack in registration order, and only forwards an error to handlers registered later than wherever it was thrown.

**Sync throw vs. async rejection in Express route handlers** — a route handler that directly does `throw new Error(...)` has always been safely caught by Express, in every version — that's a normal synchronous exception, and Express's own request-dispatch code is wrapped in a try/catch that turns it into a call to the error-handling middleware. An `async` handler that throws is different under the hood: since the function returns a Promise, throwing inside it produces a *rejected Promise*, not an exception the calling code's try/catch can see directly. Express 5 (used in this project) auto-detects when a route handler returns a Promise and attaches a `.catch()` that forwards any rejection into the same error-handling pipeline — this project's actual error scenarios (like `transactionController.js`'s `await tx.transaction.create(...)` failing) are exactly this async case, not the sync one. This wasn't automatic in Express 4 — an unhandled rejection there would have just hung the request or crashed the process, which is one reason this project is on Express 5 rather than 4.


**Single-Page Application (SPA)** — the frontend model this project uses, and the thing every other frontend concept below sits inside. Traditionally, clicking a link makes the browser throw away the current page and request a whole new HTML document from a server. An SPA loads *one* HTML file (`frontend/index.html`) plus a JavaScript bundle exactly once; from then on, JavaScript rewrites the contents of the page in place and asks the server only for *data* (JSON, via the REST API), never for new pages. That's why `index.html` here is nearly empty — just `<div id="root"></div>` and a script tag. It's also the reason this project needs CORS at all: the page is served from `localhost:5173` but its data comes from `localhost:3001`, two different origins (see the CORS entry above).

**Component** — the unit React builds UIs out of: a plain JavaScript function that returns a description of some UI. `Login`, `Home`, and `ProtectedRoute` are each one function in one file. Components nest — `App` renders `ProtectedRoute`, which renders `Home` — so the whole screen is a tree of functions, each responsible for one piece. The payoff is that a component can be understood and changed in isolation: `Home` doesn't know or care that `ProtectedRoute` checked a token before rendering it. Conceptually this is the same separation-of-concerns idea as routes vs. controllers on the backend (see above), applied to UI instead of endpoints.

**JSX** — the HTML-looking syntax inside those functions (`<div className="card">…</div>`). It is *not* HTML and not a string; it's syntax sugar that a build step compiles into ordinary JavaScript function calls that construct a description of the UI. Two consequences worth knowing early: attributes use JavaScript names rather than HTML ones (`className`, not `class`, because `class` is a reserved word in JavaScript), and anything inside `{ }` is evaluated as a JavaScript expression — which is what makes `Hello, {user.name}` in `Home.jsx` work. Because JSX isn't standard JavaScript, Node can't run a `.jsx` file directly; it has to be compiled first, which is one of the jobs Vite does.

**Props** — how a parent component passes data down to a child, written like HTML attributes. In `App.jsx`, `<ProtectedRoute><Home /></ProtectedRoute>` passes `Home` to `ProtectedRoute` as the special `children` prop, which is why `ProtectedRoute.jsx` can end with `return children`. Props flow strictly one way, parent to child, and the child treats them as read-only — a child never reaches up and edits its parent's data. That one-directional rule is what makes it possible to reason about where a given value came from.

**State, and `useState`** — props come from a parent, but a component often needs to remember something of its own that *changes over time* — what's currently typed in a text box, whether a request is in flight. That's state. `const [username, setUsername] = useState('')` in `Login.jsx` declares one piece of state with an initial value, and gives back the current value plus the only function allowed to change it. Calling `setUsername(...)` doesn't just assign a variable — it tells React this component's output is now out of date, so React re-runs the function and updates the real DOM to match. This is the central idea of React: you never write "find this element and change its text"; you change state and describe what the UI should look like for that state.

**Hook** — any function starting with `use` that lets a component tap into React features (`useState`, `useEffect`, `useContext`), or a custom one built from those (`useAuth`). Hooks have one hard rule: they must be called unconditionally at the top level of a component, never inside an `if` or a loop or after an early `return`. React matches up which hook call corresponds to which piece of remembered state purely by *call order*, so a conditional hook call would shift that order between renders and scramble the mapping. This is why `Login.jsx` calls all four `useState` hooks before the `if (status === 'authenticated')` early return, not after it.

**`useEffect`** — the hook for side effects: things that aren't "compute the UI from current state," but "reach outside the component" — fetching data, subscribing to a browser event, setting a timer. It takes a function plus a dependency array; `[]` means "run once after the component first appears." `useAuth.jsx` uses one to call `GET /api/auth/me` on mount, and a second to subscribe to the session-expired event. An effect can return a *cleanup* function, which React runs when the component goes away — that's the `removeEventListener` return in the second effect, and skipping it would leak a listener every time the provider remounted.

**StrictMode, and why effects run twice in development** — `main.jsx` wraps the app in `<StrictMode>`, which deliberately double-invokes effects in development only (it compiles out of production builds). It isn't a bug: it's a way of surfacing effects that break when run more than once, which is exactly the class of bug that otherwise appears only in rare production conditions. It's the direct reason `useAuth.jsx`'s rehydration effect carries a `cancelled` flag — without it, a discarded first run's `/me` response could still resolve and write to state after that run was thrown away.

**Context (`createContext` / `useContext`)** — props pass data one level at a time, which becomes miserable when something deep in the tree needs a value from the top: every intermediate component has to accept and forward a prop it doesn't itself care about ("prop drilling"). Context is the escape hatch — a `Provider` publishes a value, and any descendant reads it directly with `useContext`, regardless of depth. Auth is the textbook case, and the reason `AuthContext` exists here: `Home` and `ProtectedRoute` both need the current user, and nothing in between does. The custom `useAuth()` hook wraps `useContext(AuthContext)` and throws a clear error if the context is missing, which converts a confusing downstream "cannot read property of null" into a message naming the real mistake — the component was rendered outside its provider.

**Controlled input** — an `<input>` whose displayed value comes from React state and whose `onChange` writes back to that state, as in `Login.jsx`'s `value={username} onChange={e => setUsername(e.target.value)}`. The alternative (an *uncontrolled* input) lets the DOM own the value and reads it only at submit time. Controlled is the default choice here because it leaves exactly one source of truth: with the DOM holding its own copy, the two can disagree, and anything that needs to react to typing as it happens — live validation, enabling a button — has nothing reliable to read.

**Client-side routing (React Router)** — in an SPA there are no separate HTML pages to navigate between, but the URL should still change and Back/Forward should still work. React Router intercepts navigation, updates the browser's address bar via the History API without triggering a page load, and swaps which component renders. `App.jsx`'s `<Routes>` is the mapping from URL path to component. `<Navigate replace>` performs a redirect during render; `replace` matters because it *substitutes* the current history entry rather than adding one — without it, pressing Back would return to the URL that just redirected you, redirect again, and trap you in a loop.

**Route guard** — a component that wraps a protected route and decides whether to render it or redirect. `ProtectedRoute.jsx` is this project's. Worth being precise about what it is and isn't: it's a *user experience* mechanism, not a security boundary. Anyone can edit the JavaScript in their own browser to render `Home` without a token — but they'd see an empty shell, because every piece of real data still comes from an API that independently verifies the token and the role on the server (see `auth.js` / `requireRole.js`). This is the frontend counterpart to `02_ARCHITECTURE.md` §4.2's "never rely on the frontend to hide these fields": the guard exists so legitimate users aren't shown broken screens, not to keep attackers out.

**Vite, and the dev-server/build split** — Vite plays two different roles. During `npm run dev` it's a development server that compiles files (JSX → JavaScript) on demand as the browser requests them, and hot-reloads on save — roughly the frontend counterpart to nodemon. During `npm run build` it does something else entirely: bundles the whole app into a handful of minified static files in `dist/` for deployment. That build step is also a genuine correctness check, because it resolves every import in the project — a typo'd path or a syntax error fails the build rather than surfacing as a blank page later.

**`VITE_` environment-variable prefix** — backend env vars are read at runtime from the server's environment and stay on the server. Frontend code has no such privacy: it runs in the user's browser, so anything it can read, the user can read. Vite enforces this by only exposing variables prefixed `VITE_` to client code, substituting them into the bundle as literal text at build time. `VITE_API_BASE_URL` is fine to expose (it's just the API's public address). The prefix requirement exists as a guardrail: a secret sitting in the same `.env` file without the prefix simply won't be bundled, so it can't leak by accident. Verified concretely here — `localhost:3001` appears as a literal string inside the built `dist/assets/index-*.js`.

**Testing a frontend by running the real bundle in a simulated DOM** — the frontend equivalent of the backend's "mock `req`/`res`" technique (see above). `vite build` succeeding only proves the code *parses and resolves*; it says nothing about whether the app actually mounts or renders correctly. jsdom provides a JavaScript implementation of the browser's DOM APIs (`document`, `localStorage`, events) inside plain Node, so the genuine built bundle can be executed, driven programmatically, and inspected — with `window.fetch` pointed at the real backend, this exercises React mounting, the auth context, routing, and the fetch wrapper together, against a real database. One React-specific wrinkle: setting `input.value` directly doesn't trigger `onChange`, because React replaces the element's value setter to track changes itself; the workaround is to call the *prototype's* original setter and then dispatch an `input` event, which is what made the login form submittable from a test script.

**Rendering a list from an array (`.map` in JSX, and the `key` prop)** — rather than hand-writing each repeated element, JSX renders a collection by mapping an array to elements: `Home.jsx` holds a `TILES` array and does `TILES.map(({ to, label, tone, Icon }) => <Link …/>)`. The payoff is that adding Phase 2's New Order and Pack Order tiles later means appending two objects to that array, with no JSX changes at all. Each element in such a list needs a `key` prop holding a value that's stable and unique among its siblings (here the route path). React uses keys to match elements between renders — without them it can only compare by position, so inserting an item at the front makes it think every subsequent item changed, throwing away and rebuilding DOM nodes that should have been left alone. Array index is the common wrong answer for `key`, because it's exactly the thing that shifts when items are inserted or reordered.

**Conditional rendering (`&&` and the falsy-value trap)** — `{isStaff && <p className="footer-note">…</p>}` renders the paragraph only when `isStaff` is true; when false, `false` is returned, and React renders nothing for booleans, `null`, or `undefined`. This is how the Home footer note branches on role. The trap worth knowing before it bites: `&&` returns its *left* operand when that operand is falsy, so `{items.length && <List/>}` renders a literal `0` on screen when the array is empty, because `0` is falsy but is not one of the values React skips. The fix is to make the condition an actual boolean (`items.length > 0 &&`), which is why `Home.jsx` derives `const isStaff = user.role === 'STAFF'` — a comparison, always true or false — rather than testing a value directly.

**Destructuring in a component's parameters and in `.map`** — `function TruckIcon({ size = 26 })` pulls the `size` property straight out of the props object and supplies a default if the caller omitted it, instead of writing `props.size` throughout with a separate fallback. The same syntax appears in `TILES.map(({ to, label, tone, Icon }) => …)`, unpacking four properties from each array element in the parameter list. It's ordinary JavaScript rather than anything React-specific, but it's so pervasive in React code that unfamiliarity with it makes components hard to read.

**A component held in a variable, and why `Icon` is capitalised** — each `TILES` entry stores a component *itself* (`Icon: TruckIcon`), not rendered output, so the tile decides when to render it: `<Icon />`. The capital letter is load-bearing, not style. JSX treats a lowercase tag as a literal HTML element name and a capitalised one as a reference to a JavaScript variable — so `<icon />` would compile to a request for a non-existent HTML `<icon>` element and silently render nothing, while `<Icon />` compiles to a call to whatever component that variable holds. This is why every component in this project is named in PascalCase.

**`aspect-ratio` (CSS)** — `aspect-ratio: 1 / 1` on `.tile` makes each tile's height track its own computed width, keeping it square whatever the column is sized to. The older approach required a padding-percentage hack, since percentage padding resolves against width; `aspect-ratio` states the intent directly. It matters here because the same Home screen serves a narrow staff phone and the owner's desktop (§2), and this keeps the tiles square at both without any width-specific rules.

**`currentColor` (CSS)** — a keyword meaning "whatever this element's `color` property currently resolves to." Every icon in `icons.jsx` sets `stroke="currentColor"`, so placing one inside `.tile-success` (which sets `color: var(--success-text)`) makes it stroke green, and the same component inside `.tile-accent` strokes blue, with no per-icon configuration. This is what makes it practical to obey §3.1's rule that a component's background, border, and icon always share one semantic role — the role is set once on the container and the icon follows automatically.

**Semantic HTML elements (`<header>`, `<nav>`, `<p>` vs. `<div>`)** — `<div>` carries no meaning; it's a generic box. Using `<header>` for the screen header, `<nav>` for the tile grid, and `<p>` for the footer note tells browsers and assistive technology what each region *is*, which is what lets a screen-reader user jump directly to navigation instead of hearing every element in order. It costs nothing over a `<div>` and is far easier to get right while writing than to retrofit later. The same instinct as choosing `<Link>` over a click handler: use the element the platform already defines for the job.

**Testing a role-conditional UI by creating a real user for each role** — verifying that a staff-only element renders for staff and not for the owner needs an actual account of each role; asserting only against the account that happens to exist proves half the branch. The Home test creates a throwaway STAFF user through the real `POST /api/users` endpoint, logs in as it to get a genuine token, renders the app with that token, asserts the footer note appears, then deletes the user in a `finally` block so cleanup runs even if an assertion throws. This is the frontend counterpart of the backend's throwaway-fixture pattern, and the `finally` matters for the same reason it did there — a test that fails partway through shouldn't leave rows behind in a database that other tests then run against.

**Controlled `<select>`** — the same controlled-input pattern used for the Login form's text fields, applied to a dropdown: `<select value={factoryId} onChange={...}>` means React state (`factoryId`) is what actually determines which `<option>` shows as selected, not the browser's own internal selection state. This is what makes `handleFactoryChange` able to reliably reset the lookup the instant a different Factory is chosen — the change is observed as a state update, the same event React already needed to re-render the selected option, not a separate DOM query bolted on afterward.

**Derived state, computed at render time instead of stored** — `ReceiveStock.jsx` never has a `sessionReady` or `lookupDone` piece of `useState`; both are computed fresh on every render (`const sessionReady = !!factoryId && !!locationId`). This is deliberate: `sessionReady` is always fully determined by `factoryId` and `locationId`, so storing it separately would create two values that both claim to represent the same fact and could, through a missed update somewhere, disagree. Deriving it inline makes that impossible — there's only ever one source of truth (`factoryId`/`locationId`), and everything else is arithmetic on it. The rule of thumb: if a value can be calculated from other state already held, calculate it — don't duplicate it into its own `useState`.

**Race condition in UI state (a frontend instance of the backend concept)** — the backend's race-condition entry above concerns two database writes stepping on each other; the same underlying idea shows up in the UI as "an old async response arriving after the state has already moved on." If the Factory dropdown didn't reset the lookup on change, a slow-resolving lookup request kicked off under Factory A could still resolve and render *after* the user had already switched to Factory B — showing a Factory-A answer that's now describing the wrong context. Clearing `lookup` synchronously the moment the Factory changes (rather than waiting for any in-flight request to settle) closes that window entirely, the same way the backend's WHERE-clause guard closes its window by making the check and the write atomic rather than trusting an earlier read.

**Polling instead of a fixed wait, when testing against a real network** — a fixed `sleep(1200)` encodes a *guess* about how long something will take; a poll (`waitFor` in the test helpers) instead repeatedly checks whether the actual condition is already true, at a short interval, up to a generous ceiling. The first approach is either too short (flaky failures on a slow day) or wastefully long (padding every test run "just in case"); the second succeeds the moment the real thing happens and only fails if it genuinely never happens, which is the only thing a test should actually be asserting about timing. This matters specifically because these tests hit a real, remote Neon Postgres database rather than a local or mocked one — a fixed delay tuned against a fast connection is a delay tuned to fail on a slower one.

**`useRef` for a value that must survive re-renders but never trigger one** — `nextReceiptIdRef = useRef(0)` holds a counter for generating unique local ids for `receiptItems` entries. It looks similar to `useState`, but the two solve different problems: `useState` exists specifically to trigger a re-render when the value changes (that's the whole point of tracking UI state), while `useRef` gives a plain mutable box (`.current`) that persists across renders *without* causing one when it changes. A counter is a good fit for `useRef` because incrementing it is bookkeeping, not something the UI should visibly react to on its own — if it were `useState`, every commit would trigger an extra, pointless re-render purely from updating the id generator, one render before the "real" state update (`setReceiptItems`) triggers the one that actually needs to happen.

**A pure "what should the next state be" helper, called from multiple event handlers** — `finalizeActiveColor(currentStaged)` takes the array as it currently is and returns what it should become, without calling `setStagedColors` itself. Both call sites (`handleColorChange`, `handleAddToReceipt`) decide separately what to *do* with that returned array — one hands it straight to `setStagedColors`, the other uses it locally to build a receipt entry without necessarily committing it back to `stagedColors` at all. Separating "compute the next value" from "where does this value get used" is what let the same finalize logic serve two different call sites whose actual state-update needs are different, without duplicating the underlying rule (rule 51) twice.

**Filtering one array by membership in another, instead of a separate "used" flag** — `availableColors = validColors.filter(c => !stagedColors.some(s => s.colorId === c.id))` recomputes which colors are still selectable, fresh, on every render, from the two arrays that already exist. The alternative — giving each color object its own `staged: true/false` flag stored somewhere — would mean two representations of the same fact (a color's staged-ness lives both in whether it's in `stagedColors` AND in its own flag) that could disagree if one update path forgot to touch the flag. This is the same "derived state, not stored state" principle already logged for `sessionReady`/`lookupDone` in the previous task, applied to a list instead of a boolean.

**Guarding a stateful helper against its own inputs being briefly out of sync** — `finalizeActiveColor` checks `selectedColorId` and `currentSets` at the moment it's called, not at some later point, because JavaScript closures capture the values a function saw when it was *defined* (i.e., during the render that created it), not values as they change afterward. This is why `handleColorChange` calls `finalizeActiveColor` with the OLD `selectedColorId`/`currentSets` still in scope, and only calls `setSelectedColorId`/`setCurrentSets` to the new values afterward, in that exact order — swapping the order would mean the finalize step reads the color that's about to become active instead of the one being switched away from.

**Soft-delete (deactivation) vs. hard-delete** — a "delete" that doesn't actually remove the row, just flips a boolean (`isActive: false`) that the rest of the app treats as "this no longer exists for new activity." The alternative, actually running `DELETE FROM "User"`, is off the table here specifically because `Transaction.userId` is a foreign key pointing at `User.id` — deleting the row would either fail outright (the `RESTRICT` behavior already seen with `ProductSize`) or, worse, require `onDelete: Cascade`, which would delete every Transaction that user ever created along with them, destroying exactly the audit trail this whole schema exists to preserve. Soft-delete sidesteps the dilemma entirely: the row (and everything that points at it) stays exactly where it is, and "can this account still log in" becomes a runtime check (`isActive`) rather than a question of whether the record even exists.

**The fundamental limitation of stateless authentication (why "revoke this token" isn't a real operation with JWTs)** — a session-based system stores something server-side (a session ID, in a database or cache) that can simply be deleted to end that session immediately. A JWT carries everything needed to verify it — signature included — inside the token itself; the server never stores "this token is currently valid," it only re-verifies the signature and re-checks whatever it looks up by the `userId` inside. That means there is no server-side action that can invalidate one specific already-issued JWT before its `expiresIn` naturally passes — not even the server's own operator can reach out and revoke it directly. The practical workaround, used here, is to make authorization depend on data that genuinely can change server-side (`User.isActive`) and re-check that data on every request, rather than trusting anything embedded in the token itself. This is the same principle already established for why `role` is re-fetched instead of trusted from the JWT payload — applied here to a different, more consequential question: not just "does this token claim the right permissions" but "should this token be allowed to do anything at all, right now."

**A function that returns a result object instead of responding or throwing** — `verifyPin` doesn't call `res.json()`, doesn't call `next()`, and doesn't throw on failure; it returns `{ ok: true }` or `{ ok: false, status, code, message }` and lets the *caller* decide what to do with that outcome. This is a different shape from the `finalizeActiveColor` pattern logged earlier (which computed "what should the next state be") — here the function is answering "did this succeed, and if not, why," a yes/no-plus-reason question rather than a value-transformation one. Returning a plain object instead of immediately acting on the result is what let the exact same verification logic serve an Express middleware (which needs to call `next()` or `sendError`) and a controller function (which needs to keep going and do more work — hash and save a new PIN — only if the check passed). A function that reached into `res` directly, or that threw, could only ever be reused by another Express middleware in exactly the same position in a request chain — not by ordinary application code calling it as a plain function.

**Testing a code branch that's unreachable through the real system, on purpose** — sometimes two guards in the same function are ordered such that the first one always intercepts every real-world case the second one was meant to catch, as established above for `deactivateUser`'s two guards. That doesn't make the second guard's own logic untested-in-principle; it means the *normal* way of testing it (make a real request and see what happens) can't isolate it. The fix used here was to feed the function an input the live system could never actually produce — a `req.user.id` that doesn't match the real requester — specifically to force execution down the branch that ordering would otherwise always prevent. This is a deliberate, narrow use of an "impossible" input, different from testing invalid input to check error handling (e.g. a malformed request body) — the input isn't invalid, it's just a combination the real callers structurally can't create, used here purely to examine one piece of logic in isolation from another piece that happens to sit in front of it.

**A component library grown from a design brief, not built up front** — `ConfirmModal.jsx` is the first component in this project built directly from `07_UI_DESIGN_BRIEF.md`'s design-token spec (§3.4's "Confirm modal" description) before any screen that used it existed yet, rather than being extracted from one screen's markup after the fact. The brief describes this component's exact shape (centered, dark scrim, ~300px card, two full-width buttons) as something meant to be reused "for every mutation that changes persisted data" across the whole app — so building it as a generic, prop-driven component (title/body/confirmLabel/tone, not "the Manage Users deactivate dialog") from the start means the next screen that needs a destructive-action confirmation (e.g. Receive Stock's eventual "Save receipt") can reuse it directly, rather than every screen growing its own copy that then needs to be reconciled later.

**Route-level role gating vs. component-level conditional rendering, and why this project uses both together** — `ProtectedRoute`'s new `requireRole` prop stops the wrong role from ever rendering `ManageUsers`/`SetPin` at all (a redirect, before the component's own code runs); Home's `isOwner &&` / `isStaff &&` checks conditionally show or hide *pieces* of an already-rendering page. Both are doing the same underlying job — keep a UI element from showing to someone it doesn't apply to — but at different granularities: route gating answers "should this whole screen exist for this user," conditional rendering answers "should this particular button/banner exist on a screen this user is already allowed to see." Neither is a security boundary (that's still 100% server-side, per CLAUDE.md's non-negotiable rule) — both exist purely so a legitimate user's screen matches what they're actually allowed to do, instead of showing controls that would just 403 if used.

**Updating context state from outside the provider that owns it, via an exposed function** — `useAuth.jsx`'s `refreshUser` is called from `SetPin.jsx`, a completely different file, to update the `user` object that lives inside `AuthProvider`. This works because React context isn't just for *reading* shared state — the provider can also expose *functions* that know how to update its own internal state (`setUser` is never given out directly; `refreshUser` wraps it), and any descendant component can call those functions to trigger an update, the same way `login`/`logout` already worked before this task. The alternative — SetPin directly manipulating some global variable, or reaching into another component's state — isn't possible in React at all; state is only ever changed by the component that owns it (or a function that component explicitly hands out), which is what keeps "who can change this and how" traceable to one place (`useAuth.jsx`) instead of scattered across every component that happens to need to.

**Deriving a stable output order from a reference list, instead of recording interaction order** — `selectedSizes` in the New-article form is just a plain array that grows in whatever order the staff happens to tap chips (`M` then `S` then `L`, or any other sequence) — but the `sortOrder` values actually sent to the backend come from filtering a separate, fixed canonical list (`ADULT_SIZE_ORDER` / `KIDS_SIZES`) down to whatever's selected, so the stored order is always S, M, L, XL... regardless of click order. This matters because click order is an accident of how someone happened to interact with the screen, not information worth preserving — nothing about "the customer tapped XL before M" should ever affect how sizes later display in a receipt or a stock table. The general technique — keep the interaction-order list for simple toggle logic, but derive presentation/storage order separately from a canonical reference — avoids needing to maintain sorted-insertion logic in the toggle handler itself.

**A function's synchronicity is part of its contract, not an implementation detail invisible to callers** — `handleColorChange` started fully synchronous (the Matched-branch version, built two tasks ago) and became `async` in this task, once one of its branches needed to `await createBundle`. Every existing caller (`onChange={(e) => handleColorChange(e.target.value)}`) kept working unmodified, since JSX event handlers don't care whether the function they call returns a value or a Promise — but the tests built against the OLD, synchronous version broke, because they'd been written assuming "the moment this function's first visible effect appears, everything it does is finished," which stops being true the instant any part of the function can pause on an `await`. The lesson isn't really about `async` specifically — it's that changing whether a function completes its work in one tick or many is a real, breaking change to anything that observes its side effects, even when nothing about its signature or its callers' code has to change at all.

**Native activation behavior (why some DOM interactions can't be faked with a plain dispatched event)** — certain elements have a built-in browser behavior tied to specific real interactions: clicking a checkbox toggles it, clicking a submit button submits its form, clicking a link navigates. This is called "activation behavior," and it's triggered by genuine user interaction (or the element's own `.click()`/`.submit()` methods) — NOT by constructing an `Event` object and calling `dispatchEvent()` with it, even with the exact same event `type`. A manually dispatched event still fires any listeners attached to that event type (so `addEventListener('click', ...)` DOES run), but the browser's own native "and also do the built-in thing this event normally causes" step is skipped. This project has now hit this gap twice — a submit button's click not submitting its form, and a checkbox's click not toggling `.checked` — both fixed the same way: call the real method (`.click()`) or dispatch the event on the actual element that owns the native behavior (`submit` on the `<form>`, not a click on its button), rather than trying to reconstruct what the browser would have done as a side effect.

**Verifying a test interaction actually worked, not just that it appeared to** — checking `checkbox.checked === true` immediately after simulating a check is not proof the app's own state updated; it only proves the DOM node's own property reads `true` at that instant, which can be true either because React genuinely processed the change or because the property was set directly and nothing has re-rendered to correct it yet. The reliable check used here: perform an unrelated action afterward that's guaranteed to trigger a re-render (e.g. clicking the sets stepper), then check the value again — a controlled input syncs back to its real React state on every render, so if the "checked" value survives an unrelated re-render, the state genuinely changed; if it reverts, the interaction never touched React at all. This generalizes past checkboxes: any controlled input can be spot-checked this way when there's reason to distrust whether a simulated interaction really landed.

**`Map` for a client-side join, instead of `.find()` inside a loop** — `productsById = new Map(products.map(p => [p.id, p]))` builds a lookup table once, so resolving each stock row's `product.factoryId`/`product.name` afterward is a single `productsById.get(row.productId)` rather than an `products.find(p => p.id === row.productId)` re-scan of the whole products array for every single stock row. The receipt table's earlier grouping-by-productId (a previous task) used `.find()` inside `.reduce()` instead, which was fine there because it was searching the small, already-being-built *output* array (rarely more than a few groups) — this is different: it's joining two independently-fetched lists (potentially hundreds of stock rows against however many products exist) where the lookup side doesn't change while the loop runs, which is exactly the shape `Map` is for. The general rule of thumb: reach for `Map` when the same collection gets looked up repeatedly by ID inside a loop; `.find()` stays fine for one-off or small-N searches.

**Snapshotting a value into a row, instead of joining to its live source** — `Transaction.costPriceSnapshot` stores a COPY of `Product.costPrice` at the exact moment a STOCK_IN happens, rather than the payable calculation looking up `Product.costPrice` fresh every time it runs. This is a deliberate trade against the normal relational-database instinct (don't duplicate data, just join to the single source of truth) — here, duplicating it on purpose is exactly what's needed, because the single source of truth (`Product.costPrice`) is allowed to change over time, and a past receipt's cost must NOT change retroactively just because the join target did. The general pattern — copy a value into a historical record at the moment that record is created, rather than trusting a live foreign-key join to still mean the same thing later — shows up anywhere "what was true when this happened" matters more than "what's true right now": this project already does the same thing conceptually for session-item snapshotting during Receive Stock (Factory/Location/price captured into a local JS object before commit), but `costPriceSnapshot` is the first place it's a real, persisted database column rather than a client-side object field — the same idea, now with actual permanence behind it.

**Idempotent** — an action is idempotent if doing it once and doing it five times in a row leave the system in exactly the same state. `deactivateFactory` is written this way on purpose: calling it on a Factory that's already `isActive: false` doesn't return an error, it just re-applies `isActive: false` (which was already true) and responds `200` with the same shape a fresh deactivation would. The alternative — rejecting a second deactivate attempt with a `409` or similar — would be a perfectly defensible design, but it turns something that shouldn't matter (a staff member double-tapping a button, a network retry after a dropped response, two people deactivating the same record moments apart) into an error the frontend has to specially handle for no real benefit; the end state is identical either way. This is also why `deactivateUser` and all five of the newer deactivate endpoints re-check `findUnique` before updating rather than trusting the `:id` blindly — the *existence* check still 404s on a genuinely missing record, but once the record is found, flipping `isActive` to a value it might already hold is treated as a no-op success, not a special case. `POST` endpoints in this codebase are deliberately NOT idempotent by the same logic in reverse — calling `POST /api/factories` twice with the same name is supposed to fail the second time (`DUPLICATE_FACTORY`), because unlike a boolean flip, "create a new row" has no natural no-op meaning if one already exists with that identity.

**Atomic multi-write operations (why a Transfer is one endpoint, not two calls)** — a Transfer has to do five things: decrement stock at the source, increment it at the destination, create a `Transfer` record, and create two `Transaction` rows. The tempting shape is to let the frontend do it as two ordinary calls — one `STOCK_OUT` at Delhi, one `STOCK_IN` at Gurgaon — since both endpoints already exist. That is broken in a way that only shows up occasionally, which is the worst kind: if the browser loses connection, the tab closes, or the second request errors after the first succeeded, stock has left Delhi and arrived nowhere. It's simply gone, with a completely valid-looking audit trail for the half that worked.

Wrapping all five writes in `prisma.$transaction` makes the whole group **atomic** — the database either applies every one of them or none, with no observable in-between. Concretely, in `transferController.js`, if the guarded decrement finds insufficient stock and throws, the `Transfer` row created moments earlier on the same line of execution is rolled back too; nothing is left behind. Its own test asserts exactly this (a rejected transfer leaves zero orphaned `Transfer` rows, zero orphaned `Transaction` rows, and both locations' quantities untouched), because "it errored" and "it errored *cleanly*" are different claims and only the second one is worth anything.

This connects to two ideas already in this log. It's the same `$transaction` used since the first `POST /api/transactions` (stock update + Transaction insert together), just with more writes inside it. And it's why the operation gets its own dedicated endpoint rather than being composable from existing ones: atomicity is a property of a single server-side database transaction, so it cannot be reconstructed by a client making two separate HTTP calls, no matter how carefully that client is written. If two writes must not come apart, they need one door — which is also why `POST /api/transactions` permanently refuses `TRANSFER_OUT`/`TRANSFER_IN` (see the decision entry above).
